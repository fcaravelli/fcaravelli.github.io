vonNeuro: an operating system for heterogeneous, physical computing

vonNeuro is an open, experimental project to build software for heterogeneous, physical computing. It aims to support programs that use p-bits, annealers, memristive networks, neuromorphic chips, analog circuits, GPUs, ordinary CPUs and, eventually, living neural cultures, all from a single language. Its first working piece, vonNeuro Messenger, already puts real laboratory devices online: anyone can send jobs from a browser or a Python script, from VSCode and get back the measured result, with raw data included.

Why I am building this

I am a memristive-network theorist. In the past I found it surprisingly hard to get hold of hardware and simply play with it myself. I have come to believe that direct, hands-on access is key to progress. Real devices are noisy, they drift, and they have hysteresis and quirks that simulations smooth over. You only learn to program them by running code on them.

My working assumption is that the future of computing is heterogeneous. CPUs already share work with GPUs and accelerators. Next to them, a growing family of physical processors computes by letting matter relax, fluctuate or respond, rather than by stepping through instructions. These include analog circuits, memristive networks, stochastic samplers, quantum annealers and photonic devices. Some will sit next to us and some will live in the cloud. Today we write code as if it will always run on a CPU. Instead, we should be able to state what a task needs and call whichever processor suits it: a sampler for a statistical problem, an analog resource for filtering or evolving a signal in time, a relaxing system for an optimisation.

Bringing analog and stochastic computing back is not nostalgia. Physics can sample, relax to a minimum or filter a signal using far less energy than a digital machine simulating the same process. That saving is a promise to be measured, not a result to be claimed. Answers from such hardware are also statistical, so they have to be certified against what a program asked for. vonNeuro is built around both problems: deciding which device should run a task, and keeping an honest record of what it cost and how far its answer can be trusted.

The core ideas

  • One program, any substrate. A program states what it means mathematically, not which chip you own. Each backend lowers that meaning to a native physical process, such as a coupling matrix, an anneal schedule or a driven circuit, instead of pretending the device is a CPU.
  • Thermodynamic accounting. A figure in joules per task says little on its own. The goal is for every run to report its measured dissipation together with the thermodynamic lower bound it should be compared against, or to refuse the comparison when the two are not the same claim.
  • Physical includes biological. A backend qualifies if it can report its state, accept a drive and characterise its own noise. Neuronal cultures on electrode arrays, reaction networks and gene circuits can do all three, so they are part of the long-term design.

What runs today

The current bench is a proof of concept but supports the introduction of multiple hardware on the same platform. I have a first prototype meant to show that the whole loop works on real, imperfect devices:

  • 16 memristors on two 16-pin devices, behind an analog multiplexer. Their far ends are biased at 2.47 V so they can be driven both ways through a 0–5 V multiplexer. They show measured pinched hysteresis loops, and users can sweep them online at up to ±1 V to keep them healthy for the next person.
  • 16 RC coprocessors on a second multiplexer that shares address lines with the first. This makes all 32 devices addressable remotely, one guarded measurement at a time.
  • A 16-p-bit hardware sampler that runs Gibbs sampling over up to sixteen Ising spins. You load couplings, set a temperature and stream samples.
  • VOLT 0.1, an early version of the vonNeuro language. You write a small system program and run it on a chosen device, for example memristor:15 or rc:2. The device contract is checked first: a program that needs an adaptive device is refused by an RC circuit, which names the failing clause. Each reply includes the compiled graph, the source hash and the energy measured at the device terminals.
  • CPU emulators of the analog, memristor, p-bit and spiking devices, used as digital references and as fallbacks when the bench is busy.

How to get access

Create an account at vonneuro.com and open Messenger. Each account gets one hour of bench time, and more is available on request. You may see a warning that you are leaving the site. This is expected, because Messenger is currently served through a tunnel to the machine on my bench.

This is a side project, since during the day I work on quantum computing, and the bench is still a first prototype, cables and all. I am moving to a tidier soldered setup and will write about its evolution here and on LinkedIn. Feedback is very welcome.
