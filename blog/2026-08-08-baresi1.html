1. From Franco Baresi to the current crisis in science

It might seem a hard sell to connect Franco Baresi with the Fokker-Planck equation. Bear with me.

In the 1980s and 1990s, Franco Baresi was the historic captain of AC Milan. He took the helm after Gianni Rivera, whom I admit I did not remember from my childhood as I wasn't born then, and became one of the players who transformed the way Milan defended. I was an enthusiastic Milan supporter as a child, in large part because of him. If I close my eyes, I can still remember almost every player of the 1992–1995 teams.

Baresi retired in 1997, at the age of 37, and Paolo Maldini eventually inherited the captaincy. Maldini would perhaps become an even more iconic captain, but I still remember Baresi leaving. This stuff sticks with you. I recently bought an AC Milan jersey for my kid, which probably tells you how badly these childhood memories refuse to go away.

After Baresi's passing a few days ago, I first became rather nostalgic, and then rather annoyed. AC Milan was bought some years ago by a consortium. This obviously had nothing to do with Baresi, but the episode nevertheless came back to me when the management decided to have the team attend a friendly match in the US rather than the funeral.

I found this disrespectful, but more than that, I thought it was a sign of the times. One replaces history with money, and the past with the present. There is nothing wrong with the present, obviously. The problem begins when the present behaves as if nothing came before it. One of the drawbacks of capitalistic societies is that we give value to everything, while not valuing the things we should care about.

I am using Baresi here as a segue into science, because I sometimes have the same feeling about the direction in which science has been moving. Perhaps physics in particular. We seem, occasionally, to be forgetting why we started doing this in the first place.

The point was not to be the first person to upload a paper. It was not even necessarily to be the first person to obtain a result. The point was to understand something about the world. It was not about "Paper Capitalism", or "Publish or Perish".

A few days ago I attended a meeting in which a well-established scientist told a room full of young students that they should essentially not waste too much time trying to understand the world (this scientist did not use these words, but that is the message I received). Instead, the advice was to use tools such as GPT, Claude, or whatever comes next to squeeze out as many papers as possible while the present publication system still works.

Anyway, because everybody assumes that you wrote a paper with AI, the argument goes, you should just accept it and use it. Fair enough.

You could tell that the students were not particularly happy with this. They could also tell that something was changing, I thought, but were not quite sure what to make of it. I am not entirely sure what to make of it either. But it is certainly a change.

I cannot think of many crises in science that are quite like the one we are witnessing today. The closest analogy might be the moral crisis surrounding the atomic bomb in 1945. A number of Manhattan Project scientists became profoundly alarmed by what their work had produced. The Franck Report, for instance, argued for international control and suggested demonstrating the bomb rather than using it without warning.

But the scientific problem itself did not disappear. The argument moved instead to political responsibility, arms control and nonproliferation, and to the question of whether certain kinds of research should be pursued at all. These crises tend to divide scientific communities. They create the Oppenheimers and the Tellers, the people arguing on opposite sides of a technological and moral divide.

What seems different now is that the question is not only about the moral implications of a scientific discovery. It is also about the nature of scientific activity itself. I was talking to a colleague yesterday who made an interesting observation: perhaps in some ways we are moving back toward the early twentieth century, when the scientific community was smaller and the number of papers being published was much smaller too. Except, we already see a dramatic singularity in the number of papers appearing on the arXiv.

I do not know. But my response to all of this, at least for the moment, is to do a little more science.

2. The Fokker-Planck equation

Last week I attended a neuromorphic workshop in Los Alamos. In my talk, I discussed some recent work in progress with a UniPi Master's student, in which we are studying stochastic processes in memristors. That particular problem is beyond the point of this blog post, but it is where the story starts.

After the talk, I had a few discussions with Max Di Ventra about the implications of what we were finding and about some possible generalizations. I then spent part of the flight trying to understand how to deal with a Fokker-Planck equation under cyclic control, which was part of the question, essentially.

More precisely, I wanted to understand how to characterize, and then actually compute, the NESS — the non-equilibrium steady state — when the generator itself changes periodically in time, analytically. Thankfully, I had Risken with me, so I had a look.

I had a few hours on the airplane, which is occasionally still one of the few places where one is forced to think about a problem for more than ten consecutive minutes. Eventually the structure became fairly clear. I will not discuss the specific memristive model here. Instead I want to use it as an excuse to give a short introduction to periodic Fokker-Planck problems. I will provide a general overview.

And since we are about to discuss periodic driving, there is an appropriate soundtrack: Fatboy Slim, Ya Mama (Push The Tempo).

2.1 From stochastic dynamics to the Fokker-Planck equation

Let us begin with a fairly general stochastic system. Consider an \(n\)-dimensional state \[ \mathbf{x}(t) = \bigl(x_1(t),\ldots,x_n(t)\bigr), \] described, in the Itô convention, by \[ dx_i = a_i(\mathbf{x},t)\,dt + \sum_{\alpha=1}^{m} b_{i\alpha}(\mathbf{x},t)\,dW_\alpha(t). \]

The functions \(a_i(\mathbf{x},t)\) describe the deterministic drift, while the coefficients \(b_{i\alpha}(\mathbf{x},t)\) tell us how the different sources of noise enter the dynamics. The Wiener increments satisfy \[ \langle dW_\alpha\rangle=0, \qquad \langle dW_\alpha dW_\beta\rangle = \delta_{\alpha\beta}\,dt. \]

A single solution of this stochastic equation gives us one trajectory. The Fokker-Planck description asks a different question: how does the entire probability distribution \[ p(\mathbf{x},t) \] evolve?

The answer is the forward Kolmogorov, or Fokker-Planck, equation: \[ \frac{\partial p(\mathbf{x},t)}{\partial t} = -\sum_i \frac{\partial}{\partial x_i} \left[ a_i(\mathbf{x},t)p(\mathbf{x},t) \right] + \frac{1}{2} \sum_{i,j} \frac{\partial^2}{\partial x_i\partial x_j} \left[ D_{ij}(\mathbf{x},t)p(\mathbf{x},t) \right], \] with diffusion matrix \[ D_{ij}(\mathbf{x},t) = \sum_\alpha b_{i\alpha}(\mathbf{x},t) b_{j\alpha}(\mathbf{x},t). \]

One point is worth making immediately. There is no reason for either the drift or the diffusion matrix to be independent of time. If they are time independent and the drift has a gradient form, then under the appropriate conditions the asymptotic stationary distribution belongs to the familiar exponential, or Gibbs-like, family.

We can, however, perfectly well have \[ a_i=a_i(\mathbf{x},t), \qquad D_{ij}=D_{ij}(\mathbf{x},t). \] The equation still works. What changes is our usual notion of a stationary state.

2.2 Probability as a conserved fluid

A useful way of reading the Fokker-Planck equation is as a continuity equation, \[ \frac{\partial p}{\partial t} = -\nabla\cdot\mathbf{J}, \] where \(\mathbf{J}\) is the probability current.

In components, \[ J_i(\mathbf{x},t) = a_i(\mathbf{x},t)p(\mathbf{x},t) - \frac{1}{2} \sum_j \frac{\partial}{\partial x_j} \left[ D_{ij}(\mathbf{x},t)p(\mathbf{x},t) \right]. \]

Probability therefore behaves, formally, like a conserved fluid moving through state space. Under the appropriate boundary conditions, \[ \frac{d}{dt} \int p(\mathbf{x},t)\,d\mathbf{x} = 0. \] Start with a normalized density and it remains normalized.

This way of writing things also makes the difference between equilibrium and non-equilibrium particularly clear. In equilibrium, when detailed balance holds, one normally has \[ \mathbf{J}_{\mathrm{eq}}(\mathbf{x})=0. \]

A non-equilibrium steady state is more interesting. In the ordinary, time-independent case one may have \[ \frac{\partial p}{\partial t}=0 \] while at the same time \[ \mathbf{J}\neq0. \]

The density is stationary, but probability may still be circulating through state space.

Under periodic control, the corresponding condition is instead \[ p(\mathbf{x},t+T) = p(\mathbf{x},t), \] or, equivalently, \[ \int_t^{t+T} \frac{\partial p}{\partial s}\,ds = 0. \]

This distinction becomes particularly important when the forcing itself is cyclic.

2.3 The Fokker-Planck operator

From now on it is convenient to hide the derivatives inside an operator. Let \[ \mathcal{L}_t \] denote the time-dependent Fokker-Planck generator. Then \[ \frac{\partial p}{\partial t} = \mathcal{L}_t p. \]

Explicitly, \[ \mathcal{L}_t p = -\sum_i \partial_{x_i}(a_i p) + \frac{1}{2} \sum_{i,j} \partial_{x_i}\partial_{x_j} (D_{ij}p). \]

If the problem is autonomous, \[ \mathcal{L}_t=\mathcal{L}, \] then formally \[ p(t)=e^{t\mathcal{L}}p(0). \] The usual stationary state is simply a zero mode, \[ \mathcal{L}p_{\mathrm{ss}}=0. \]

This is the standard Fokker-Planck exercise: find the null space of the generator, impose the boundary conditions and normalization, and obtain the stationary distribution.

With a genuinely time-dependent generator this is no longer the right question. There need not exist a single time-independent distribution satisfying \[ \mathcal{L}_t p_{\mathrm{ss}}=0 \] at every instant.

Instead, introduce the propagator \[ p(t) = \mathcal{U}(t,t_0)p(t_0), \] where \[ \mathcal{U}(t,t_0) = \mathcal{T} \exp\left( \int_{t_0}^{t}\mathcal{L}_s\,ds \right). \]

The \(\mathcal{T}\) is the time-ordering operator. We need it because, in general, \[ [\mathcal{L}_{t_1},\mathcal{L}_{t_2}] \neq0. \] The order in which the evolution occurs matters.

2.4 What does "steady" mean under cyclic driving?

Now we finally arrive at the problem that was bothering me on the airplane.

Suppose the control is periodic with period \(T\), so that \[ \mathcal{L}_{t+T} = \mathcal{L}_t. \] The generator never becomes stationary. Nevertheless, after many cycles the system can forget its initial condition.

What does a steady state mean in this case?

The answer is that the asymptotic distribution does not need to become constant. It only needs to repeat: \[ p_{\mathrm{ps}}(\mathbf{x},t+T) = p_{\mathrm{ps}}(\mathbf{x},t). \]

During a cycle the distribution can breathe, move and distort. One period later, however, it comes back to the same distribution. This is the periodic non-equilibrium steady state.

The natural object to consider is therefore not the instantaneous generator, but the evolution over one complete cycle: \[ \mathcal{M} \equiv \mathcal{U}(T,0) = \mathcal{T} \exp\left( \int_0^T \mathcal{L}_t\,dt \right). \]

I will call \(\mathcal{M}\) the cycle operator. One also encounters names such as period propagator or monodromy operator.

The periodic steady-state problem is then simply \[ { \mathcal{M}p_0=p_0. } \]

We have traded the usual zero-eigenvalue problem for the generator for an eigenvalue-one problem for the full-cycle evolution.

Once \(p_0\) is known at some chosen phase of the driving cycle, the rest follows from

\[ p_{\mathrm{ps}}(t) = \mathcal{U}(t,0)p_0, \qquad 0\leq t\lt T. \]

By construction,

\[ p_{\mathrm{ps}}(T) = \mathcal{M}p_0 = p_0. \]

2.5 Stroboscopic stationarity

I find another way of saying this particularly useful. Imagine observing the system only at \[ 0,\;T,\;2T,\;3T,\ldots \] so that every observation is made at exactly the same phase of the external drive.

Then \[ p_{n+1} = \mathcal{M}p_n. \] We have turned a continuously driven stochastic system into a discrete dynamical system acting on probability distributions.

Under the usual assumptions that make the dynamics mixing, repeated application of the cycle operator gives \[ \mathcal{M}^{n}p_{\mathrm{initial}} \longrightarrow p_0. \]

So the system is not stationary if I stare at it continuously, but it becomes perfectly stationary if I look at it stroboscopically.

This is the first useful way to think about cyclic NESS states. It is general. But it does not yet tell us how to compute one efficiently. For that, the Fourier structure of a periodic drive gives us another route.

3. Solving for NESS states with cyclic control

Let us now try to actually solve the problem using a Fourier expansion. The idea is to exploit the fact that the generator is periodic in time. Let me call this the "Risken trick".

The cycle-operator formulation above is completely general, but it does not exploit the fact that many experimentally relevant controls are very simple functions of time. Suppose, for instance, that the control is harmonic: \[ V(t) = V_0+V_1\cos(\omega t), \qquad T=\frac{2\pi}{\omega}. \]

Suppose also that the Fokker-Planck generator depends affinely on the control parameter \(V\). Then we can write \[ \mathcal{L}(t) = \mathcal{L}_{V_0} + V_1\cos(\omega t)\mathcal{L}_{B}. \]

Let us absorb the factor \(V_1/2\) into \[ \mathcal{K} \equiv \frac{V_1}{2}\mathcal{L}_{B}. \] Since \[ \cos(\omega t) = \frac{1}{2} \left( e^{i\omega t}+e^{-i\omega t} \right), \] the generator becomes \[ \mathcal{L}(t) = \mathcal{L}_{V_0} + \mathcal{K}e^{i\omega t} + \mathcal{K}e^{-i\omega t}. \]

Now use the one fact we already know about the asymptotic state: it is periodic. We can therefore expand it in Fourier modes, \[ p(x,t) = \sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}} p_n(x)e^{in\omega t}. \]

Because \(p(x,t)\) is real, \[ p_{-n}(x) = p_n(x)^*. \] Probability normalization gives \[ \int p_0(x)\,dx=1, \qquad \int p_n(x)\,dx=0, \qquad n\neq0. \]

Substituting the Fourier series into the Fokker-Planck equation gives \[ in\omega p_n = \mathcal{L}_{V_0}p_n + \mathcal{K} \left( p_{n-1}+p_{n+1} \right). \]

Define \[ \mathcal{A}_n \equiv \mathcal{L}_{V_0} - in\omega\mathcal{I}. \] Then \[ { \mathcal{A}_n p_n + \mathcal{K}p_{n-1} + \mathcal{K}p_{n+1} = 0. } \]

This is the key simplification.

We started with a time-dependent partial differential equation. In Fourier space it has become an infinite tridiagonal hierarchy. The harmonic \(p_n\) talks only to \(p_{n-1}\) and \(p_{n+1}\). Nothing further away.

There is a simple reason for this. The drive itself contains only the frequencies \(+\omega\) and \(-\omega\), and therefore multiplication by the drive can shift the Fourier index only by one unit.

3.1 From differential operators to matrices

The quantities \(p_n\) are still functions of \(x\), so the equation above is an operator recurrence rather than an ordinary matrix equation. We can make the matrix structure explicit by choosing a basis, \[ p_n(x) = \sum_j c_{jn}\phi_j(x), \] or simply by discretizing the \(x\)-coordinate.

Let \[ \mathbf{c}_n = (c_{1n},c_{2n},\ldots)^T \] contain the coefficients of the \(n\)-th temporal harmonic.

The operators then become matrices. Writing \[ \mathbf{A}_n = \mathbf{L}_0 - in\omega\mathbf{I}, \] the hierarchy becomes \[ { \mathbf{A}_n\mathbf{c}_n + \mathbf{K}\mathbf{c}_{n-1} + \mathbf{K}\mathbf{c}_{n+1} = 0. } \]

If we wrote the complete system at once, we would obtain the block-tridiagonal Floquet problem \[ \begin{pmatrix} \ddots & \ddots & & & \\ \ddots & \mathbf{A}_{-1} & \mathbf{K} & & \\ & \mathbf{K} & \mathbf{A}_0 & \mathbf{K} & \\ & & \mathbf{K} & \mathbf{A}_1 & \ddots \\ & & & \ddots & \ddots \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} \vdots\\ \mathbf{c}_{-1}\\ \mathbf{c}_0\\ \mathbf{c}_1\\ \vdots \end{pmatrix} = 0. \]

At this point we could truncate the Fourier hierarchy at some \(|n|\leq N\) and throw the whole thing at a sparse solver. There is nothing wrong with doing that.

But there is a nicer trick, discussed by Risken in the context of Fokker-Planck equations. Because the hierarchy is tridiagonal, we can eliminate the higher harmonics recursively and convert the problem into a matrix continued fraction.

3.2 The Risken trick

Consider the positive-frequency branch. Write \[ \mathbf{c}_n = \mathbf{S}_n\mathbf{c}_{n-1}. \] Then automatically \[ \mathbf{c}_{n+1} = \mathbf{S}_{n+1}\mathbf{c}_n = \mathbf{S}_{n+1} \mathbf{S}_n \mathbf{c}_{n-1}. \]

Substitute this into \[ \mathbf{A}_n\mathbf{c}_n + \mathbf{K}\mathbf{c}_{n-1} + \mathbf{K}\mathbf{c}_{n+1} = 0. \] We obtain \[ \mathbf{A}_n \mathbf{S}_n\mathbf{c}_{n-1} + \mathbf{K}\mathbf{c}_{n-1} + \mathbf{K} \mathbf{S}_{n+1} \mathbf{S}_n \mathbf{c}_{n-1} = 0. \]

Factoring out \(\mathbf{c}_{n-1}\), \[ \left( \mathbf{A}_n + \mathbf{K}\mathbf{S}_{n+1} \right) \mathbf{S}_n + \mathbf{K} = 0. \]

Therefore \[ { \mathbf{S}_n = - \left[ \mathbf{A}_n + \mathbf{K}\mathbf{S}_{n+1} \right]^{-1} \mathbf{K}. } \]

Or, putting back the definition of \(\mathbf{A}_n\), \[ { \mathbf{S}_n = - \left[ \mathbf{L}_0 - in\omega\mathbf{I} + \mathbf{K}\mathbf{S}_{n+1} \right]^{-1} \mathbf{K}. } \]

And that is essentially the whole trick.

The relation for \(\mathbf{S}_n\) contains \(\mathbf{S}_{n+1}\), which contains \(\mathbf{S}_{n+2}\), which contains \(\mathbf{S}_{n+3}\), and so on. In other words, it is a matrix continued fraction.

To make this explicit, define \[ \mathbf{F}_n \equiv \mathbf{A}_n + \mathbf{K}\mathbf{S}_{n+1}. \] Since \[ \mathbf{S}_{n+1} = -\mathbf{F}_{n+1}^{-1}\mathbf{K}, \] we find \[ { \mathbf{F}_n = \mathbf{A}_n - \mathbf{K} \mathbf{F}_{n+1}^{-1} \mathbf{K}. } \]

Iterating, \[ \mathbf{F}_n = \mathbf{A}_n - \mathbf{K} \left[ \mathbf{A}_{n+1} - \mathbf{K} \left[ \mathbf{A}_{n+2} - \mathbf{K} \left[ \mathbf{A}_{n+3} - \cdots \right]^{-1} \mathbf{K} \right]^{-1} \mathbf{K} \right]^{-1} \mathbf{K}. \]

Finally, \[ { \mathbf{S}_n = -\mathbf{F}_n^{-1}\mathbf{K}. } \]

There is nothing mystical about the continued fraction. We are simply integrating out the higher Fourier harmonics one after another.

If you prefer linear-algebra language, it is repeated block Gaussian elimination, or equivalently a sequence of Schur complements, applied to the infinite Floquet matrix above.

3.3 Closing the fraction

Of course, on a computer we cannot descend from \(n=\infty\). So we pick a sufficiently large harmonic cutoff \(N\) and impose \[ \mathbf{c}_{N+1}=0. \]

At the top of the hierarchy, \[ \mathbf{S}_N = -\mathbf{A}_N^{-1}\mathbf{K}. \] We then iterate downward: \[ \mathbf{S}_{N-1}, \mathbf{S}_{N-2}, \ldots, \mathbf{S}_1. \]

As usual, \(N\) is not magic. Increase it until the answer stops changing. That gives a direct numerical convergence test.

Once \(\mathbf{S}_1\) has been obtained, \[ \mathbf{c}_1 = \mathbf{S}_1\mathbf{c}_0, \] and all the higher positive harmonics follow recursively: \[ \mathbf{c}_n = \mathbf{S}_n \mathbf{S}_{n-1} \cdots \mathbf{S}_1 \mathbf{c}_0. \]

The negative-frequency branch is treated in exactly the same way. For a real Fokker-Planck generator, \[ \mathbf{A}_{-n} = \mathbf{A}_n^*, \] and reality of the probability distribution implies \[ \mathbf{c}_{-n} = \mathbf{c}_n^*. \]

In particular, \[ \mathbf{c}_{-1} = \mathbf{S}_1^*\mathbf{c}_0. \]

3.4 A closed equation for the averaged NESS

We can now eliminate every harmonic except \(n=0\). The zeroth equation of the hierarchy is \[ \mathbf{L}_0\mathbf{c}_0 + \mathbf{K}\mathbf{c}_{-1} + \mathbf{K}\mathbf{c}_1 = 0. \]

Using \[ \mathbf{c}_1 = \mathbf{S}_1\mathbf{c}_0, \qquad \mathbf{c}_{-1} = \mathbf{S}_1^*\mathbf{c}_0, \] gives \[ { \left[ \mathbf{L}_0 + \mathbf{K} \left( \mathbf{S}_1+\mathbf{S}_1^* \right) \right] \mathbf{c}_0 = 0. } \]

Since \[ \mathbf{S}_1 = -\mathbf{F}_1^{-1}\mathbf{K}, \] we can write the same equation entirely in continued-fraction form: \[ { \left[ \mathbf{L}_0 - \mathbf{K}\mathbf{F}_1^{-1}\mathbf{K} - \mathbf{K} \left(\mathbf{F}_1^*\right)^{-1} \mathbf{K} \right] \mathbf{c}_0 = 0. } \]

I like this expression because it shows what the method has actually accomplished. We began with an infinite collection of coupled temporal harmonics. We have now integrated all of them out and obtained an effective stationary equation for the zero-frequency component \(\mathbf{c}_0\).

Notice that \(\mathbf{c}_0\) is not the full periodic state. It is its time average. But once \(\mathbf{c}_0\) is known, every other Fourier component follows from the recursive matrices \(\mathbf{S}_n\).

The last ingredient is just probability normalization. If \(\mathbf{w}\) is the quadrature vector representing integration over \(x\), then \[ \mathbf{w}^T\mathbf{c}_0=1. \]

Solve the effective equation together with this normalization, reconstruct the harmonics, and finally write \[ p(x,t) = \sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}} p_n(x)e^{in\omega t}. \] That is the periodic NESS.

3.5 Linear response falls out as a limit

There is a nice check on the construction. If the modulation \(V_1\) is small, then \(\mathbf{K}=O(V_1)\), and the first level of the continued fraction gives \[ \mathbf{S}_1 = - \left( \mathbf{L}_0 - i\omega\mathbf{I} \right)^{-1} \mathbf{K} + O(V_1^2). \]

Hence \[ \mathbf{c}_1 = - \left( \mathbf{L}_0 - i\omega\mathbf{I} \right)^{-1} \mathbf{K}\mathbf{c}_0 + O(V_1^2). \]

This is just the familiar linear-response resolvent. The poles of the operator \[ \left( \mathbf{L}_0-i\omega\mathbf{I} \right)^{-1} \] encode the relaxation times, phase lag and frequency dependence of the response.

So the matrix continued fraction is not some unrelated solution method. It contains linear response as its first approximation. Keeping the whole fraction simply resums the repeated transitions \[ 0\rightarrow1\rightarrow2\rightarrow3\rightarrow\cdots \] and their negative-frequency counterparts.

In this sense it gives a non-perturbative extension of the ordinary small-amplitude response calculation.

And this was more or less the observation I was trying to reach on the airplane: a periodically driven Fokker-Planck problem, which initially looks like an awkward explicitly time-dependent PDE, can instead be viewed as a stationary problem in an enlarged Floquet space. Once you see that structure, the rest becomes rather mechanical.

4. Conclusions

This has been a somewhat long road from Franco Baresi to matrix continued fractions, but I think there is a connection after all. The technical calculation above is not new mathematics. Floquet theory is old, the Fokker-Planck equation is old, and Risken's continued-fraction machinery is old. What was new to me was realizing that these pieces fit together very naturally for the problem I happened to be thinking about.

That is, in fact, a large part of what I still enjoy about doing science. One encounters a problem, becomes confused by it, spends some time rearranging it, remembers something written decades ago, and suddenly the problem looks different. The point is not that nobody has ever seen any of the ingredients before. The point is that now I understand why they fit together.

This is also why I remain uneasy with the idea that the objective of science should become the production of the largest possible number of papers with the smallest possible amount of thought in between. AI will certainly change how we calculate, search, write code and perhaps even how we discover things. Some of those changes will be extremely useful. But producing an answer and understanding an answer are still different activities.

Perhaps that distinction will disappear one day. For the moment, I do not think it has.

So my response to the current crisis is not particularly sophisticated. Keep reading old things. Keep trying to understand the equations. And occasionally spend a few hours on an airplane thinking about a Fokker-Planck equation.

Most importantly: watch an old video of Franco Baresi playing football. It is still a joy to watch. In particular, watch the Milan team of the early 1990s, with Baresi, Maldini, Desailly, Costacurta, and the rest.
