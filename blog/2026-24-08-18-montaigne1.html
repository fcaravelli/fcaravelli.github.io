de Montaigne, Peter Thiel, and the AI Rat Race

August 9th, 2026

A few years ago I left my position at Los Alamos. In retrospect, it was a good thing. At the time, it did not feel like one.

Leaving a prestigious institution is strange because prestige has a peculiar gravitational field. From the inside, one can be unhappy, uncertain, or simply aware that something is no longer working. From the outside, however, leaving looks irrational. There are positions one is supposed to want. Once one has obtained one, voluntarily walking away from it can feel less like a decision and more like stepping off a ledge.

I have been thinking about this recently after watching, almost by accident, two very different discussions. I was reading about the life of Michel de Montaigne. The algorithm then recommended to me a video in which there was Peter Thiel's famous Stanford lecture, Competition Is for Losers. I realized after that the person who introduced him to the students/crowd was actually Sam Altman.. yuck.

The surprising thing is that both de Montaigne and Thiel are, in some sense, saying the same thing: get out of the "rat race" (life!).

But they mean almost opposite things by it. The temporal closeness by which I discovered some peculiarities of these two very different thinkers made me realize that the current AI boom is producing a new kind of rat race. Thinking about the effects of AI on Science and the scientific community, I realized that the Montaignean and Thielian perspectives are both relevant, but in very different ways. Next week who knows what I will discover.

1. Montaigne leaves the race

I have been reading about de Montaigne because of some analogies I saw with myself. Michel de Montaigne was born in 1533 into a wealthy family near Bordeaux (unlike mine). He studied law, became a magistrate, and eventually served in the Parlement of Bordeaux. These were not marginal positions in sixteenth-century France. They belonged to precisely the world of status, office, political connections, reputation, and public advancement that ambitious men were expected to pursue.

Then, at the age of thirty-eight, Montaigne withdrew. Funnily enough, this is the same age at which I decided to quite my LANL job.

He returned to his family estate, retired to a tower containing his library, and began writing what became known today as the Essais. Note that essai in french means an attempt, a trial, an experiment. Montaigne was not constructing a philosophical system in the manner of someone trying to establish the final architecture of reality. He was trying things out on himself, in practice.

His subject was, famously, de Montaigne. In the sense, he was a proto-psychologist, a proto-anthropologist, and a proto-sociologist. He was trying to understand the human condition by observing himself and the people around him. He was trying to understand the human condition in a way that was not abstract, but practical.

This can sound narcissistic until one reads him. What makes de Montaigne important is almost the opposite. He distrusts grandiosity. He distrusts certainty. He distrusts the strange human tendency to turn one's position in the social hierarchy into the measure of one's existence.

His famous question was Que sais-je? — what do I know?

This was written in a century saturated with people who were quite certain that they knew. Europe was being torn apart by political and religious conflict. de Montaigne's response was not indifference, but a form of epistemic modesty. Human beings are fallible, real, inconsistent creatures. In a way, the Nirvana, or philosophical wisdom begins partly by noticing this. In the same way in which a crazy person might be the only sane one in a room full of people who are all pretending to be sane.

What I found attractive about de Montaigne is the way he approached his learning process. There is a wonderful Montaignean image that I keep returning to. He describes the need for an arrière-boutique, literally a room behind the shop: a private interior place that does not belong to one's profession, public identity, obligations, or status. AI makes me feel, right now, a form of arriereboutiqueism is needed.

This is what makes his retirement philosophically interesting. He did not leave one competition in order to dominate another one. He was trying to recover the experience of living before life had been completely translated into achievement. In a way, this is what a few centuries later happened. The scientific revolution first, the industrial revolution later, put humans at the center of the universe. Right now, humans are being put at boundaries at such universe. Imagine where God is placed right now, in the age of AI. I am not sure I want to know.

Near the end of his life, Montaigne's ideal was wonderfully unheroic: he wanted death to find him planting cabbages, unconcerned with death and even more unconcerned with the unfinished state of his garden. I just bought a piece of land with that idea in mind. How many parallels!

There is something almost offensive about this attitude to a modern professional culture. The garden is unfinished. The work is unfinished. The CV is unfinished. The career is unfinished. This sense of laisser-faire, of not needing to finish, is almost a form of anti-capitalist rebellion.

And that is fine, maybe. But we were talking about Thiel.

2. Thiel leaves the competition

The discussion above about de Montaigne is not meant to be a historical comparison. de Montaigne was a sixteenth-century Frenchman. Peter Thiel is a twenty-first-century American. The two cannot be compared directly. One might dismiss Thiel as a Silicon Valley entrepreneur, a libertarian, or a billionaire. I do think that Thiel is a thinker as well. Peter Thiel's argument about "competition is for losers" might seem a bit Montaignesque, but it is not. It is a very different argument.

His claim that "competition is for losers" is usually read as a statement about monopoly. And at the business level, that is exactly what it is. A company operating in perfect competition has little ability to capture the value it creates. A company that does something genuinely unique can occupy a market in which direct comparison becomes much less meaningful.

But the more interesting part of the lecture comes near the end, when Thiel stops talking about firms and begins talking about people.

Competition, he argues, is psychologically attractive because it validates us. In practice, for a scientist: if many intelligent people are trying to enter the same profession, win the same fellowship, publish in the same journal, enter the same university, or solve the same fashionable problem, then pursuing it feels important.

In this sense, the crowd supplies the proof. In particular, what struck me was his discussion about scientists. He said explicitly that scientists are often motivated by the desire to be recognized by their peers. They are not the richest people in the world. We, the scientists, seem to lack that ability and the story of the scientist as a lonely genius is a myth.

Thiel's objection is that human beings are intensely mimetic. He uses that word exactly in the way I use it: he was a student of Girard at Stanford. We look around, discover what other ambitious people desire, and begin desiring it ourselves. Eventually we become extremely good at climbing ladders without remembering why that particular wall deserved a ladder.

He tells the story of leaving a prestigious New York law firm after seven months and three days. A colleague told him that seeing Thiel leave was reassuring because he had not realized it was possible to escape. Thiel's joke is that escaping required nothing more complicated than walking through the front door and not returning.

(I understand that story differently now than I would have before leaving Los Alamos. Institutions can become psychologically closed long before they become physically closed. This is for instance what happened to Bell Labs in the 1980s to the late 1990.)

de Montaigne and Thiel a saying the same thing for two very distinct purposes. de Montaigne says: stop needing to win.

Thiel says: stop playing games in which winning means doing slightly better than everyone standing next to you. He uses the example of a chess tournament in which the winner is the person who wins the most games. de Montaigne withdraws from competition because life is finite. Thiel withdraws from competition because competition produces sameness. Capitalism is the essense of competition, yet this form of regulated competition cannot be accepted as a model for a successful business. One wants to leave competition to enjoy life. The other wants to avoid competition to take it all. In a way, Thiel is a more ruthless de Montaigne. He wants to win, but he wants to win in a way that does not require him to compete with others. He wants to be the only one in the room. If you want, this is the essential part of the Silicon Valley recipe.

One searches for an inner room full of peace. The other searches for an empty market, in a way another form of peace. One leaves the crowd in order to live something ordinary completely. The other leaves the crowd in order to create something extraordinary that the crowd has not noticed.

And then AI arrived.

3. AI moves the scientific distribution

Whatever one thinks about the current AI boom, this is an unusually interesting time to be alive.

In Science, AI has changed the effective level at which an individual researcher can operate. Innovations needed to be developed by hand. Someone can move much more quickly through an unfamiliar literature. Symbolic calculations that once consumed an afternoon can be checked in minutes. Code can be written, translated, debugged, and explained at a speed we have not seen before. One can interrogate methods belonging to a neighboring field without first spending six months becoming locally fluent in its dialect.

The natural picture is that the distribution of scientific capability has moved to the right. That sounds unambiguously good. And locally, it is. Science does not care about who or what developed that piece of knowledge.

But there is an equilibrium problem hiding inside the obvious productivity gain.

Science is not carried out by one isolated researcher receiving a better tool while everyone else remains fixed. Everyone receives the tool. Authors adapt. Referees adapt. Students adapt. Journals adapt. Standards adapt.

So the relevant question is not merely:

$$\text{How much more can I do with AI?}$$

It is also:

$$\text{What happens when everybody can do more?}$$

This is where the problem starts to look like a small statistical-mechanical model.

4. A Gaussian model of scientific accessibility

Let me use a simple model to illustrate the problem. In a way, it also applies to the state of scientific publication industry pre-AI, but it is more relevant now. I will make a few assumptions that are not strictly true, but they make the mechanism transparent. Let us assign every scientist an effective field-specific sophistication level $X$. This is not IQ. It is simply a compressed variable containing mathematical preparation, familiarity with a literature, technical experience, conceptual background, judgment, and all the other ingredients required to absorb a particular piece of work.

Suppose, for simplicity, that the sophistication level is distributed according to a Gaussian distribution:

$$X\sim\mathcal N(\mu,\sigma^2).$$

This assumption might be modified to include a long tail, but the Gaussian is a good starting point that allows me to make calculations transparents. Now, the output of a scientist is a piece of work, a manuscript, etc. A paper has technical depth $d$. Imagine first a sharp absorption threshold: a researcher can productively use the paper only when

$$X\geq d.$$

The meaning of this statement is that literally, only a handful of people to the right of the sophistication can understand what the paper is about.T his is obviously another idealization, but it makes the mechanism transparent.

Let me use a normalized quantity $z$ to denote the number of standard deviations above the mean that the paper sits:

$$z=\frac{d-\mu}{\sigma},$$

In turn, this means that the fraction of the scientific population capable of absorbing the paper is

$$A(d)=1-\Phi(z) =1-\Phi\!\left(\frac{d-\mu}{\sigma}\right).$$

Now let introduce another quantity $q(d)$, which denotes the intrinsic scientific value of a contribution of depth $d$, conditional on somebody actually being able to understand and use it. If there are $N$ potential scientific readers, a very simple model of realized recognition is

$$C(d)=Nq(d) \left[ 1-\Phi\!\left(\frac{d-\mu}{\sigma}\right) \right].$$

What the formula in practice says is that there are two competing effects.

Increasing $d$ can produce better science. But it simultaneously moves the paper farther into the upper tail of the population capable of reading it. Now, in discussions with colleagues I mentioned often the following: "the best paper is the one that is read by the most people". For that reason, a paper should be centered close to the peak of the sophistication distribution. Doing things that are too deep, while not a waste of time, it is not the most efficient way to get recognition. Some people do understand this. This is a very different statement than the one made by Montaigne, who was not concerned with recognition at all. If you want to have a career, one has to move in Science as Peter Thiel describes: move into a small field and dominate it. Be it.

In the mathematical formula, one has to see this tradeoff exactly, take logarithms left and right:

$$\log C(d) = \log N+\log q(d) +\log\left[1-\Phi(z)\right].$$

Differentiating with respect to $d$ gives

$$ \frac{d}{dd}\log C(d) = \frac{q'(d)}{q(d)} - \frac{1}{\sigma} \frac{\phi(z)}{1-\Phi(z)}. $$

We now introduce the inverse Mills ratio (I discovered this in a paper by economist David Card, but it is a classical object in statistics):

$$ m(z)=\frac{\phi(z)}{1-\Phi(z)}. $$

Then

$$ \frac{d}{dd}\log C(d) = \frac{q'(d)}{q(d)} - \frac{1}{\sigma}m(z). $$

This equation contains almost the entire story. The first term is the proportional gain from moving deeper into the scientific frontier. The second is the "accessibility penalty": how quickly the potential population of readers disappears as the paper becomes more demanding.

Now take the particularly simple formula for the depth

$$q(d)=e^{\gamma d},\qquad \gamma>0.$$

Then

$$\frac{q'(d)}{q(d)}=\gamma,$$

and the citation-optimal sophistication $d^*$ satisfies

$$ m(z^*)=\gamma\sigma. $$

Therefore we finally get to a function

$$ { d^* = \mu+\sigma m^{-1}(\gamma\sigma) }. $$

There is no universal rule saying that the optimal paper sits one standard deviation above the mean scientist. Its location depends on the scientific return to depth, $\gamma$, and on the width of the sophistication distribution, $\sigma$. However, it says in formulae what I have been saying in words to younger (and older) colleagues. Tails are for the brave, not the careerist. The question is again what we do Science for.

Yet, the formula gives a useful distinction that gets lost when we talk casually about "better science." Scientific quality and scientific recognition are not the same object. Recognition is quality filtered through a population capable of absorbing it.

5. A Red Queen effect

We now try to introduce AI into the model. The simplest way to do this is to assume that AI increases the effective sophistication of every scientist by a fixed amount. This is not strictly true, but it is a good starting point. The model can be made more complicated, but the essential mechanism is already visible in this simple version.

I want to use here a term from biology: the Red Queen effect. The name comes from Lewis Carroll's Red Queen: one has to keep running just to stay in the same place. InThrough the Looking Glass, the Red Queen tells Alice that in her country, "it takes all the running you can do, to keep in the same place." The Red Queen effect I use here is a phenomenon in evolutionary biology. The analogy here is direct. While AI makes scientists more capable, but if the scientific frontier moves with them, the relative distance to that frontier does not shrink.

Note: You can find a good Wiki page about the Red Queen effect , here evolutionary biology.

Let us model AI simply as follows. Let $a$ represent the additional effective scientific capability supplied by AI. A researcher whose unaided sophistication is $X$ now operates at

$$X^{AI}=X+a.$$

A paper of depth $d$ can therefore be absorbed when

$$X+a\geq d,$$

or

$$X\geq d-a.$$

For our Gaussian population, the accessible fraction becomes

$$ A(d;a) = 1-\Phi\!\left( \frac{d-a-\mu}{\sigma} \right). $$

Thus

$$ C(d;a) = N e^{\gamma d} \left[ 1-\Phi\!\left( \frac{d-a-\mu}{\sigma} \right) \right]. $$

Define

$$ z=\frac{d-a-\mu}{\sigma}. $$

Repeating the previous derivative,

$$ \frac{\partial}{\partial d}\log C = \gamma-\frac{1}{\sigma}m(z). $$

The optimum therefore obeys exactly the same condition,

$$m(z^*)=\gamma\sigma.$$

Hence $z^*$ is constant, and

$$ \boxed{ d^*(a) = a+\mu+\sigma m^{-1}(\gamma\sigma) }. $$

Consequently,

$$ \boxed{ \frac{\partial d^*}{\partial a}=1 }. $$

This is the result that I find both beautiful and slightly disturbing.

If AI gives everyone one additional unit of effective scientific capability, the citation-maximizing paper becomes one unit more sophisticated.

The relevant gap is

$$d^*(a)-a.$$

But from the equation above,

$$ d^*(a)-a = \mu+\sigma m^{-1}(\gamma\sigma), $$

which does not depend on $a$.

Therefore the accessible fraction is also unchanged:

$$ A(d^*(a);a) = 1-\Phi\!\left[m^{-1}(\gamma\sigma)\right]. $$

In words, everybody becomes more capable.

The frontier moves by exactly the same amount.

Nobody becomes relatively closer to it.

This is a scientific Red Queen effect: we run faster in order to remain in the same place! Notice what happened (in the model). AI genuinely improved human capability. Nothing in the calculation denies that. The disappearance of the accessibility gain comes from the endogenous response of scientific production.

The mistake would be to hold $d$ fixed while changing $a$. But we, the scientists, do not hold $d$ fixed.

6. A tragedy of the scientific commons

The previous result is not yet a tragedy. It is merely an arms race in which the gain from AI is dissipated.

The stronger possibility appears when we remember that scientific attention is scarce.

Knowledge itself is not a rival resource. My understanding of an equation does not prevent you from understanding it. Attention is different. There are only so many papers a person can read, talks one can attend, manuscripts one can referee, and ideas one can seriously think about.

Suppose there are $M$ papers, and paper $i$ has depth $d_i$. Let its share of scientific attention be

$$ v_i = \frac{e^{\rho(a)d_i}} {\sum_{j=1}^{M}e^{\rho(a)d_j}}. $$

The parameter $\rho(a)$ measures how strongly apparent frontier sophistication attracts attention. This is a modeling assumption, not a claim that every scientific community rewards complexity in exactly this way. But it captures a recognizable incentive: technical sophistication can itself become a signal of frontier status.

Realized impact is now

$$ Y_i = N e^{\gamma d_i} \left[ 1-\Phi\!\left( \frac{d_i-a-\mu}{\sigma} \right) \right]v_i. $$

The author chooses $d_i$. Differentiating the log-attention term gives

$$ \frac{\partial\log v_i}{\partial d_i} = \rho(a)(1-v_i). $$

The private first-order condition is therefore

$$ \gamma - \frac{1}{\sigma} m\!\left( \frac{d_i-a-\mu}{\sigma} \right) + \rho(a)(1-v_i) = 0. $$

In a symmetric equilibrium,

$$v_i=\frac{1}{M}.$$

Define

$$ R(a)=\rho(a)\left(1-\frac{1}{M}\right). $$

The Nash condition becomes

$$ \frac{1}{\sigma} m\!\left( \frac{d_N-a-\mu}{\sigma} \right) = \gamma+R(a). $$

Equivalently,

$$ m(z_N) = \sigma[\gamma+R(a)], $$

so

$$ \boxed{ d_N(a) = a+\mu+ \sigma m^{-1}\!\left( \sigma[\gamma+R(a)] \right) }. $$

Compare this with the previous expression. Without competition for attention, the final term was constant. Now it can move too.

Differentiate. Since

$$ m(z_N)=\sigma[\gamma+R(a)], $$

implicit differentiation gives

$$ m'(z_N)\frac{dz_N}{da} = \sigma R'(a). $$

Therefore

$$ \frac{dz_N}{da} = \frac{\sigma R'(a)}{m'(z_N)}. $$

Since

$$d_N=a+\mu+\sigma z_N,$$

we obtain

$$ \boxed{ \frac{dd_N}{da} = 1+ \frac{\sigma^2R'(a)}{m'(z_N)} }. $$

For the Gaussian inverse Mills ratio,

$$ m'(z)=m(z)[m(z)-z]>0. $$

Thus, if AI also increases the intensity of competition for attention,

$$R'(a)>0,$$

then

$$ \boxed{ \frac{dd_N}{da}>1. } $$

This is much stronger than the Red Queen result.

The frontier no longer moves to the right at the same speed as our capabilities. It moves faster.

To see what this does to accessibility, note that the relevant standardized gap is

$$ z_N= \frac{d_N-a-\mu}{\sigma}. $$

Since $dz_N/da>0$ whenever $R'(a)>0$,

$$ \frac{d}{da}[1-\Phi(z_N)] = -\phi(z_N)\frac{dz_N}{da} <0. $$

We have arrived at the paradox:

AI makes every scientist individually more capable, while the equilibrium scientific literature can become relatively less accessible.

This is a tragedy of the commons because the scarce common resource is attention. An individual scientist can gain an advantage by producing something that looks more technically advanced, contains another derivation, another simulation, another model, another layer of formal machinery. If only one person does this, perhaps that person obtains more attention. If everybody does it, the relative advantage disappears. What remains is the additional complexity. This is precisely where Thiel re-enters the story. Competition makes us better at the dimension on which we are competing. The uncomfortable question is whether that dimension was worth optimizing in the first place. In practice, AI makes competion more likely, flattens the distribution of capability, and makes the dimension of competition more extreme.

AI may make this problem unusually severe because it lowers the cost of participating in the competition. If everyone can generate more derivations, more code, more simulations, more literature review, more polished prose and more papers, the equilibrium response need not be leisure.

It may simply be that the expected amount of all of those things increases. One might happen is that those groups who already had the attention gains more attention, and those who had less attention lose more attention. In practice, contrarily to what I said in a few posts ago, the AI boom is not making the scientific distribution more Gaussian. It is making it more heavy-tailed. The Red Queen effect is more severe than I expected. La corsa sul posto.
