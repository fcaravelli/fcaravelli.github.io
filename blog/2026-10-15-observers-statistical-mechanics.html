Whose entropy?

Observers, coarse graining, and where the information goes

October 15th, 2026

I have had this question in my head, and recently wanted to get it out. I took the opportunity for a festschrift in honor of a great scientist to write this down(shh it's a secret!). Recently, I have also talked about this at the Smoluchowski and DICE symposiums. I have not written on this blog in some time, the paper was still being worked on, and there were a few things I wanted to settle before writing about it here. Now the cat is out of the bag, the paper has been submitted, and I am happy to talk about it.

A small side story before getting into this. Yesterday (I start to work on this post on October 7th) the Nobel Prize in Physics was announced, and it went to Francis Halzen for his contributions to IceCube and the discovery of high-energy astrophysical neutrinos. Speaking about IceCube, he mentioned “perseverance, talent and luck”. To me, that is a pretty good description of what science is or should be about.

I particularly like that luck is included. A joke among students in Italy is that Exam $= mc^2$, which stands Exam = memory multiplied by luck square (luck written as culo in italian). You can work on a problem for years, know what you are doing, and still depend on circumstances you cannot control. When something finally works, it is easy to tell its story as if it had been heading there all along. I appreciate hearing somebody acknowledge how uncertain it was while they were actually doing it. There is, clearly, an attack on a certain type of science, and we are currently ongoing a paradigmatic shift. But this story was a thing in my head for a good chunk of the last few years.

Anyway, back to observers, which truly is what IceCube is about. When we say that the entropy of a system has increased, what did we actually observe? Is entropy a measure of disorder like people write about.. ? Did we measure its energy? Its density? A few correlations? And would somebody who measured more assign the same entropy change?

Statistical mechanics has lived with this question for a long time. Usually we settle on a macroscopic description and then get on with the calculation. In practical terms, we write a Langevin mesoscopic equation, or a Fokker-Planck equation, and then compute the entropy production based on this assumption. But we forget, somehow, that there is an underlying idea behind it, which is what we actually observed.

>

Fair enough. But I wanted to keep the choice of description inside the calculation, and ask whether two observers of the same microscopic evolution could compare their answers in a precise way.

The submitted paper is called Observers, Entropy and Integral Fluctuation Theorems in the Mori–Zwanzig–Grabert formalism. What follows is my attempt to explain the problem, the philosophical motivation, and some of the mathematics without turning the post into another paper.

1. What does an observer keep?

Let us start from a familiar, historical, scenario. Consider an elongated Brownian particle in a liquid. If the particle is isotropic, then the diffusivity tensor is isotropic. If not, its mobility along its long axis can differ from its mobility in the transverse direction. IF both types of particles are present in the same system, then measuring one versus the other can imply different type of fluctuations. Somebody who measures both position and orientation can resolve that anisotropy. The Einstein relation can then be tensorial,

\[ \mathbf D = k_B T\,\boldsymbol\mu. \]

Somebody who records only position, over times long compared with orientational relaxation, may infer an effectively scalar diffusion coefficient. This requires the orientations to sample an isotropic stationary distribution. Without that averaging and separation of timescales, leaving out orientation need not produce isotropic diffusion.

Both people are looking at two particles in the same liquid. Their descriptions differ because their records differ. Even the distinction between an organized motion and a fluctuation depends on which variables are retained. A contribution that is predictable when orientation is known can become part of the effective noise when it is omitted.

This is the sense in which I use the word observer. It can be a person, a measurement protocol, or a simulation that saves five columns of data and discards the rest. For the construction in the paper, the data are expectation values of selected phase-space observables, e.g. projections down from the full microscopic ensemble. The retained data are the only information we keep. We also need a rule that turns the retained data into a probability density. That second ingredient matters quite a lot.

2. Zeh and the choice of relevance

I got some inspiration, or rather, a direction for this work reading a very inspiring book by Zeh. Dieter Zeh calls what we just described the relevance concept. In his discussion of Zwanzig's formalism, coarse graining includes both smoothing a distribution and discarding correlations. Local thermodynamic descriptions omit instead nonlocal correlations; information can move into those correlations while the fine-grained ensemble entropy stays constant. Which information is relevant depends on the description and on what local observers can access.

Zeh also insists on a dynamical question. Selecting relevant variables does not establish an autonomous evolution for them. One needs suitable boundary conditions and assumptions about the return of discarded information. In his account, the initial absence of irrelevant information and its subsequent transfer into increasingly complicated correlations help explain the thermodynamic arrow. Choosing a coarse graining by itself does not explain why that arrow points towards the future.

My reading of this is that we should write the resolution into our thermodynamic statements. A macroscopic record cannot distinguish every microscopic distribution. Calling two such distributions the same state is a useful operation, but it is an operation we have performed. The entropy assigned after that operation should remember how it was done.

There is a familiar tension here. Hamiltonian evolution preserves the fine-grained Gibbs entropy,

\[ S_{\mathrm{fg}}[\rho_t] =-k_B\int d\Gamma\,\rho_t(\Gamma)\ln\rho_t(\Gamma), \qquad \frac{dS_{\mathrm{fg}}}{dt}=0. \]

This is due, in essence, to Liouville's theorem. The phase-space volume of a set of points is preserved under Hamiltonian transport. Yet the entropy used in a reduced description can grow. The evolving density develops structure that the reduced description does not retain. We need two densities to discuss this properly: the transported density and the one reconstructed from the retained information. In 2023 I co-organized a workshop with Michael Saccone, Luis Pedro Garcia Pintos and Cristiano Nisoli (we called.. Conclave!) on thermodynamics and information. Chris Jarzynski was invited and I remember asking exactly why this was the case and whether there were fluctuation theorems reconciling this discrepancy. He said "I thought I had resolved this years ago, but now I don't remember how". I asked then whether he thought this was a good direction to dig, he nodded, and here we are years later (I got lost, tried a few things, got back to this with a different approach).

That is where I start. Throughout the classical calculation, phase-space volumes are measured in units of a fixed microscopic reference cell, so the quantities inside logarithms are dimensionless. The same reference measure is used when comparing observers.

3. From measurements to a statistical state

Let us try to fix first the notation. Let \(\Gamma\) denote a point in phase space, and let \(\rho_t\) be the microscopic ensemble density. We choose phase-space observables as \(A_1(\Gamma),\ldots,A_n(\Gamma)\). The retained data is

\[ a_i(t)=\int d\Gamma\,\rho_t(\Gamma)A_i(\Gamma). \]

A finite collection of means does not determine \(\rho_t\). There are generally many densities with those same means. The observer therefore needs a reconstruction map,

\[ \mathcal O=\bigl(\{A_i\},\;a\mapsto\bar\rho(\cdot;a)\bigr), \qquad \int d\Gamma\,\bar\rho_t A_i=a_i(t). \]

I use maximum entropy for the thermodynamic part of the paper. We maximize the Shannon–Gibbs entropy over the normalized densities compatible with the data. For a regular interior solution, this gives

\[ \bar\rho_t(\Gamma) =\exp\!\left[-\Phi(t)-\sum_i\lambda_i(t)A_i(\Gamma)\right], \qquad \Phi(t)=\ln\int d\Gamma\,e^{-\sum_i\lambda_i(t)A_i(\Gamma)}. \]

The multipliers are chosen to reproduce the measured means. Retaining mean energy produces a canonical family. Retaining means and second moments of continuous variables produces a Gaussian family, when the corresponding maximum exists. Different retained data lead to different families of reconstructed states.

Jaynes gives us the inference rule; Grabert lets the reconstructed state follow the changing macroscopic data. The entropy assigned to this observer is

\[ S(t)=-k_B\int d\Gamma\,\bar\rho_t\ln\bar\rho_t, \qquad \frac{S(t)}{k_B}=\Phi(t)+\sum_i\lambda_i(t)a_i(t). \]

The choice of maximum entropy deserves to be visible. Normalization and moment matching already define a much more general reconstruction. Some of the identities below hold at that level. Maximum entropy supplies the extra geometry that makes the entropy comparison particularly clean. It does not guarantee that our selected variables predict the future well.

4. What we leave out comes back as memory

There is a reason for doing this within Mori–Zwanzig–Grabert theory. The reconstruction also defines a projection of the microscopic dynamics. First, it simplifies a lot of the calculations, as the relevant density comes out naturally as an exponential family. This is also a limitation, as it does not come out of the dynamics itself, but it is, in this sense, imposed. However, it is a very natural choice, and it is the one that makes the entropy comparison particularly clean, and it also directly provides a projector operator that can be used in molecular dynamics simulations. For a differentiable, moment-matched family, the Grabert projector acting on a phase-space function \(X\) is

\[ P(t)X=\langle X\rangle_{\mathrm{rel},t} +\sum_j[A_j-a_j(t)] \int d\Gamma\,\frac{\partial\bar\rho_t}{\partial a_j}X, \qquad Q(t)=1-P(t). \]

It leaves the retained observables unchanged: \(P(t)A_i=A_i\), and \(P(t)^2=P(t)\). For the regular maximum-entropy family, with invertible covariance matrix \(C\), the same expression becomes

\[ P(t)X=\langle X\rangle_{\mathrm{rel},t} +\sum_{j,k}\delta A_j(C^{-1})_{jk} \langle\delta A_kX\rangle_{\mathrm{rel},t}, \qquad \delta A_i=A_i-a_i(t). \]

This projects onto the resolved span using the instantaneous relevant density as its weight. A Mori projection uses a fixed reference weight. A Zwanzig conditional expectation retains every function of the selected variables. Keeping a few moments and keeping their entire joint distribution are different resolutions.

For autonomous Hamiltonian dynamics and time-independent observables, the exact projected equation has the form

\[ \dot A_i(t)=v_i(t)+\sum_j\Omega_{ij}(t)\delta A_j(t) +\int_0^t ds\, \left[K_i(t,s)+\sum_j\varphi_{ij}(t,s)\delta A_j(s)\right] +F_i(t,0). \]

The details of the kernels are in the paper, and it will be clear once it appears in press. What matters here is the division into drift, memory, and fluctuating force. The omitted variables still influence the retained ones. Eliminating them produces dependence on the earlier history and on unresolved initial information.

Changing the observer changes this division. Keeping an additional slow variable can move part of what looked like memory into the resolved dynamics. The microscopic evolution has continued all along; our way of organizing its consequences has changed.

The entropy identities I will discuss do not require us to replace the memory kernel by a delta function, or to close this equation. This is useful because memory is often precisely what makes a reduced description interesting.

5. An entropy change along a trajectory

Let us now discuss the transported microscopic density and the reconstructed density along a trajectory We follow one microscopic trajectory, \(\Gamma_t=\Phi_t(\Gamma_0)\). At each endpoint the observer assigns a relevant density. Define the dimensionless functional

\[ \Sigma(\Gamma_0;t) =\ln\frac{\bar\rho_0(\Gamma_0)}{\bar\rho_t(\Gamma_t)}. \]

Equivalently, if the stochastic relevant entropy is \(s_{\mathrm{rel}}(\Gamma,t)=-k_B\ln\bar\rho_t(\Gamma)\), then \(k_B\Sigma\) is its endpoint change along that trajectory. An individual \(\Sigma\) fluctuates. It need not equal the change of the ensemble entropy \(S\).

Its average has a particularly useful meaning. Let me introduce the Kullback–Leibler divergence,

\[ D(\rho\|\sigma)=\int d\Gamma\,\rho\ln\frac{\rho}{\sigma}. \]

Hamiltonian transport gives \(\rho_t(\Gamma_t)=\rho_0(\Gamma_0)\). Insert that equality into the endpoint ratio and average over the actual initial ensemble. Whenever the divergences are finite,

\[ \langle\Sigma(t)\rangle_{\rho_0} =D(\rho_t\|\bar\rho_t)-D(\rho_0\|\bar\rho_0). \]

This identity needs no maximum-entropy assumption. It measures the change in the discrepancy between the transported ensemble and its reconstruction. If our reconstruction was exact at the start, \(\rho_0=\bar\rho_0\), it simplifies to

\[ \langle\Sigma(t)\rangle_{\mathrm{rel},0} =D(\rho_t\|\bar\rho_t)\geq0. \]

Maximum entropy lets us go one step further. Since \(\ln\bar\rho_t\) is affine in the retained observables, moment matching makes its average identical under \(\rho_t\) and \(\bar\rho_t\). Consequently,

\[ D(\rho_t\|\bar\rho_t) =\frac{S(t)-S_{\mathrm{fg}}}{k_B}, \qquad \langle\Sigma(t)\rangle_{\rho_0} =\frac{S(t)-S(0)}{k_B}. \]

The second equality holds for any initial preparation with finite quantities in this moment-matched exponential construction. Its sign becomes nonnegative when the initial preparation is relevant. The microscopic evolution preserves information; the reconstructed state fails to retain some of its developing structure. The divergence puts a number on that failure.

6. The observer dependent integral fluctuation theorem (IFT)

Now we want to show that IFT is independent of the observer. As a matter of fact, what follows is a special case of Seifert 2005, but now with a careful attention to observers and how to operationally measure the fluctuations. Suppose we prepare the system in \(\bar\rho_0\), and first take a regular family with full support. The endpoint functional satisfies an integral fluctuation theorem in the following sense,

\[ \left\langle e^{-\Sigma(t)}\right\rangle_{\mathrm{rel},0}=1. \]

The proof is short enough to be shown here. First, we substitute the definition, cancel the initial density, and change variables along the Hamiltonian flow:

\[ \begin{aligned} \left\langle e^{-\Sigma(t)}\right\rangle_{\mathrm{rel},0} &=\int d\Gamma_0\,\bar\rho_0(\Gamma_0) \frac{\bar\rho_t(\Phi_t\Gamma_0)}{\bar\rho_0(\Gamma_0)}\\ &=\int d\Gamma_0\,\bar\rho_t(\Phi_t\Gamma_0)\\ &=\int d\Gamma_t\,\bar\rho_t(\Gamma_t)=1. \end{aligned} \]

The last step uses Liouville volume preservation and normalization. There is no reversed experiment in this proof, and no assumption that the reduced dynamics is Markovian. Maximum entropy is needed for the preceding identification with reconstructed entropy, rather than for this normalization argument.

If the initial relevant density vanishes on part of phase space, let \(S_0=\{\Gamma:\bar\rho_0(\Gamma)>0\}\). The unit right-hand side requires the final relevant density to give no weight outside \(\Phi_t(S_0)\). Otherwise,

\[ \left\langle e^{-\Sigma(t)}\right\rangle_{\mathrm{rel},0} =1-\ell_t, \qquad \ell_t=\int_{\mathcal X\setminus\Phi_t(S_0)} d\Gamma\,\bar\rho_t(\Gamma). \]

A sharp macrostate can have exactly this problem: its final coarse reconstruction spreads probability over points that the initial preparation never reaches. Normalizing both endpoint densities does not remove that missing support.

With compatible support, Jensen's inequality gives \(\langle\Sigma\rangle_{\mathrm{rel},0}\geq0\). This compares a later time with the chosen preparation. It does not prove \(\dot S\geq0\) at every intermediate time. To restart the same argument at a later instant we would need the microscopic density to equal its reconstruction there, too. Generally it does not.

In fact, the general identity makes the role of the boundary condition rather transparent. If the reconstruction is exact at the final time instead, the mean becomes \(-D(\rho_0\|\bar\rho_0)\). The sign follows from where we impose the simple state. Explaining why physical preparations select one direction remains part of the arrow-of-time problem.

7. Two observers, one system

Now, let us try to go beyond a single observer. Now let observer \(a\) retain everything observer \(b\) retains, and some additional observables. Thus \(a\) is in this sense the finer observer. Both compute their moments from the same microscopic ensemble, and both use maximum entropy with the same reference measure.

It helps to think in the space of probability densities. Define

\[ \mathcal F_o(t)=\left\{ \omega\geq0:\int d\Gamma\,\omega=1,\quad \int d\Gamma\,\omega A_i^{(o)}=a_i^{(o)}(t) \text{ for all }i\right\}. \]

Each point in this set is a whole density, rather than a phase-space point. Additional constraints shrink the set. The actual density lies in both feasible sets, while their entropy maximizers can differ:

\[ \mathcal F_a(t)\subseteq\mathcal F_b(t) \quad\Longrightarrow\quad S^{(a)}(t)\leq S^{(b)}(t). \]

There is less freedom to spread probability when more information has to be reproduced. The entropy gap is exactly

\[ \mathcal I_{b:a}(t) :=D\!\left(\bar\rho_t^{(a)}\|\bar\rho_t^{(b)}\right) =\frac{S^{(b)}(t)-S^{(a)}(t)}{k_B}\geq0. \]

The reason is moment matching again. The logarithm of the coarser density involves only its retained observables, and the finer density reproduces their means. The same mechanism gives the information geometry's Pythagorean identity,

\[ D(\rho_t\|\bar\rho_t^{(b)}) =D(\rho_t\|\bar\rho_t^{(a)}) +D(\bar\rho_t^{(a)}\|\bar\rho_t^{(b)}). \]

To check it, subtract the right-hand side from the left. What remains is \(\int d\Gamma\,(\rho_t-\bar\rho_t^{(a)}) \ln(\bar\rho_t^{(a)}/\bar\rho_t^{(b)})\). The log-ratio is affine in the finer observable list, so the integral vanishes. No Euclidean right triangle is required; the cancellation comes from the matched constraints.

On one trajectory, define their disagreement by

\[ \begin{aligned} \delta(\Gamma_0;t)&=\Sigma^{(b)}(\Gamma_0;t)-\Sigma^{(a)}(\Gamma_0;t)\\ &=\ln\frac{\bar\rho_t^{(a)}(\Gamma_t)}{\bar\rho_t^{(b)}(\Gamma_t)} -\ln\frac{\bar\rho_0^{(a)}(\Gamma_0)}{\bar\rho_0^{(b)}(\Gamma_0)}. \end{aligned} \]

Average both assignments over the same physical preparation. The result is the relation I wanted:

\[ \boxed{\langle\delta(t)\rangle_{\rho_0} =\mathcal I_{b:a}(t)-\mathcal I_{b:a}(0).} \]

Their mean disagreement is the change in the information gap between their reconstructions. A coarser observer always assigns at least as much instantaneous entropy, but its entropy change need not be larger. The gap itself can shrink. When the initial reconstructions coincide, the coarser mean change is larger by the final gap.

This is a statement about genuinely nested constraints. Knowing \(\langle A\rangle\) does not generally determine \(\langle f(A)\rangle\). An observable being a function of another does not, by itself, order observers who retain only their means.

8. A free particle is enough

Let us do a calculation. Take a free particle on the line,

\[ H=\frac{p^2}{2m},\qquad p_t=p_0,\qquad q_t=q_0+\frac{t}{m}p_0. \]

Prepare an uncorrelated Gaussian ensemble with zero means and initial variances \(\sigma_{q,0}^2\) and \(\sigma_{p,0}^2\). The evolution shears the Gaussian in phase space. Directly expanding \(q_t^2\) and \(q_tp_t\) gives

\[ \begin{aligned} \sigma_q^2(t)&=\sigma_{q,0}^2+\frac{t^2}{m^2}\sigma_{p,0}^2,\\ \sigma_p^2(t)&=\sigma_{p,0}^2,\\ \operatorname{Cov}(q_t,p_t)&=\frac{t}{m}\sigma_{p,0}^2. \end{aligned} \]

Observer \(b\) keeps the two means and variances. Its maximum-entropy reconstruction is a product of Gaussian marginals. Observer \(a\) also keeps \(\langle qp\rangle\), so it reconstructs the full joint Gaussian. Since the actual ensemble remains Gaussian, \(a\)'s reconstruction is exact at all times.

The covariance determinant makes such distinction clear. Let \(C_a\) and \(C_b\) be the covariance matrices of the two reconstructions. Then:

\[ \det C_a(t)=\sigma_{q,0}^2\sigma_{p,0}^2, \qquad \det C_b(t)=\left(\sigma_{q,0}^2+ \frac{t^2}{m^2}\sigma_{p,0}^2\right)\sigma_{p,0}^2. \]

The Gaussian entropy now contains \(\tfrac12\ln\det C\). Thus the finer entropy stays constant, while the coarser one grows. If \(r(t)\) denotes the correlation coefficient, their gap is

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathcal I_{b:a}(t) &=-\frac12\ln[1-r(t)^2]\\ &=\frac12\ln\left(1+ \frac{t^2\sigma_{p,0}^2}{m^2\sigma_{q,0}^2}\right). \end{aligned} \]

Here this is also the actual mutual information between position and momentum, because the finer Gaussian is the actual ensemble. For a non-Gaussian ensemble, the same Gaussian reconstruction gap would not generally equal its true mutual information.

In this example \(\Sigma^{(a)}=0\) on every trajectory, and

\[ \langle\Sigma^{(b)}(t)\rangle_{\rho_0} =\mathcal I_{b:a}(t). \]

No heat is exchanged. The mean kinetic energy is constant. The free particle has simply developed a position–momentum correlation that one reconstruction keeps and the other removes. This small example tells us rather clearly what the observer's entropy production measures.

9. The fluctuations of disagreement

Comparing means leaves out the trajectory fluctuations. Could we take the two individual fluctuation theorems and conclude that \(\langle e^{-\delta}\rangle=1\)? No. Even when both theorems hold under one common preparation, an average of a ratio does not follow from the two separate averages:

\[ e^{-\delta}=\frac{e^{-\Sigma^{(b)}}}{e^{-\Sigma^{(a)}}}. \]

More generally, each observer's unit IFT is guaranteed under its own relevant preparation. Those preparations need not coincide. Our comparison of the mean assignments used a common physical ensemble without requiring either reconstruction to be exact initially.

We can nevertheless express the exponential disagreement using observable endpoint statistics. Stack the two observable lists into a vector \(\mathbf A\), and define

\[ \ln\frac{\bar\rho_s^{(a)}}{\bar\rho_s^{(b)}} =\Theta(s)-\boldsymbol\mu(s)\cdot\mathbf A(\Gamma,s), \quad \Theta=\Phi^{(b)}-\Phi^{(a)}, \quad \boldsymbol\mu=(\boldsymbol\lambda^{(a)},-\boldsymbol\lambda^{(b)}). \]

Center the observables with their actual means, \(\Delta\mathbf A_s=\mathbf A(\Gamma_s,s)-\langle\mathbf A_s\rangle_{\rho_0}\), and introduce the joint endpoint cumulant generator,

\[ K_{0,t}(\boldsymbol\eta,\boldsymbol\xi) =\ln\left\langle e^{\boldsymbol\eta\cdot\Delta\mathbf A_t+ \boldsymbol\xi\cdot\Delta\mathbf A_0} \right\rangle_{\rho_0}. \]

For nested maximum-entropy observers, the centered disagreement is \(\delta-\langle\delta\rangle =-\boldsymbol\mu(t)\cdot\Delta\mathbf A_t +\boldsymbol\mu(0)\cdot\Delta\mathbf A_0\). Exponentiating and averaging therefore gives

\[ \left\langle e^{-\delta}\right\rangle_{\rho_0} =\exp\!\left[-\Delta\mathcal I_{b:a} +K_{0,t}\bigl(\boldsymbol\mu(t),-\boldsymbol\mu(0)\bigr)\right], \qquad \Delta\mathcal I_{b:a}=\mathcal I_{b:a}(t)-\mathcal I_{b:a}(0). \]

This is the relational identity in the paper, where the exponential moment is finite and nonzero. It separates the change in the mean information gap from a contribution involving joint endpoint fluctuations. It does not impose an additional universal normalization on the disagreement.

Writing \(\mathcal G_{0,t}=-\ln\langle e^{-\delta}\rangle\), the cumulant expansion, when it converges, starts as

\[ \mathcal G_{0,t} =\langle\delta\rangle-\frac12\operatorname{Var}(\delta) +\frac16\kappa_3(\delta)-\cdots. \]

If \(\delta\) is Gaussian, the expansion stops at the variance. Gaussian phase-space variables alone do not guarantee this: our observable list can contain quadratic quantities such as \(qp\). Also, the variance involves correlations between the two endpoints. Two instantaneous reconstructions are enough to fix the mean gap; their separate endpoint statistics are generally insufficient to fix the fluctuations of its trajectory change.

10. Where do heat and work enter?

The free-particle calculation is a good reason to be careful with the words entropy production. The endpoint functional measures reconstruction loss. A physical dissipated-work or reservoir-entropy interpretation needs the corresponding physical setup and reference states.

For example, prepare a canonical state at inverse temperature \(\beta_0\), then drive the Hamiltonian while the system is thermally isolated. Use the fixed-temperature canonical reference family

\[ \rho_t^{\mathrm{ref}}(\Gamma) =\frac{e^{-\beta_0H(\Gamma;\Lambda_t)}}{Z_t}, \qquad F_t=-\beta_0^{-1}\ln Z_t. \]

The Hamiltonian endpoint energy difference is the work \(W=H(\Gamma_t;\Lambda_t)-H(\Gamma_0;\Lambda_0)\). The reference endpoint ratio is consequently

\[ \ln\frac{\rho_0^{\mathrm{ref}}(\Gamma_0)} {\rho_t^{\mathrm{ref}}(\Gamma_t)} =\beta_0(W-\Delta F), \qquad \langle e^{-\beta_0W}\rangle_{\mathrm{eq},0} =e^{-\beta_0\Delta F}. \]

This recovers Jarzynski's equality. An observer who instead matches the actual mean energy reconstructs a canonical family with a generally changing multiplier \(\beta_G(t)\). Its endpoint functional obeys the appropriate IFT, but need not equal \(\beta_0(W-\Delta F)\). Nor does an energy-fitting multiplier establish that the driven system is in thermal equilibrium.

The distinction appears in another Pythagorean split,

\[ \beta_0\bigl(\langle W\rangle-\Delta F\bigr) =D(\rho_t\|\bar\rho_{G,t}) +D(\bar\rho_{G,t}\|\rho_t^{\mathrm{ref}}). \]

Part of the mean dissipated work is information omitted by the energy-only reconstruction. The rest is the distance of that reconstruction from the fixed-temperature equilibrium reference.

A deterministic thermostat requires a different adjustment. For a smooth invertible, compressible flow, let \(J_t=|\det(\partial\Gamma_t/\partial\Gamma_0)|\). Since \(d\Gamma_t=J_t\,d\Gamma_0\), the functional becomes

\[ \Sigma_{\mathrm{th}} =\ln\frac{\bar\rho_0(\Gamma_0)}{\bar\rho_t(\Gamma_t)}-\ln J_t. \]

The Jacobian restores the change-of-variables proof and the information-gap identity. Interpreting it as reservoir entropy requires specifying the thermostat. For Nosé–Hoover dynamics, with a shared canonical Gaussian weight for the thermostat variable, the thermostat-energy boundary terms cancel and one obtains

\[ \Sigma_{\mathrm{NH}} =\ln\frac{\bar p_0(x_0)}{\bar p_t(x_t)}+\beta Q_B, \qquad \beta=(k_BT)^{-1}. \]

Here \(x\) denotes the physical system variables, and \(Q_B\) is heat counted positive into the bath. The IFT retains its extended initial-preparation and support conditions. If two observers use the same thermostat weight and follow the same trajectory, the explicit reservoir contribution cancels from their disagreement. The common Jacobian likewise cancels for two descriptions of any one compressible flow. The thermostat still affects the trajectories and their statistics.

11. What this leaves us with

Why should statistical mechanics depend on observers? The accepted answer is because the macroscopic state is specified through retained information. A mean energy, a density profile, or a covariance matrix each distinguishes different classes of microscopic distributions. An entropy assigned from such data belongs to the reconstruction built from them. What we tried to do here is to extend it beyond statistical mechanics and make the observer explicit in the trajectory-level fluctuation theorems.

Once the observables, reference measure, reconstruction, and preparation are specified, the comparisons above are definite calculations. Two people using that same specification obtain the same answer. Two people using different resolutions can quantify how their answers differ. This is what I find appealing about keeping the observer explicit: it gives us something to compare beyond the word subjective.

There is also a useful limit. A description retaining the full ensemble density has \(\bar\rho_t=\rho_t\), and its Hamiltonian endpoint functional is zero on every trajectory. This means retaining the whole probability density, rather than knowing one exact phase-space point. It is the fully resolved ensemble limit of this construction.

A partition observer gives the familiar coarse-grained version. If it retains the probabilities \(p_m\) of fixed cells with volumes \(\Omega_m\), maximum entropy makes the density uniform inside each cell, and

\[ \frac{S_{\mathcal P}}{k_B} =-\sum_m p_m\ln p_m+\sum_m p_m\ln\Omega_m. \]

This becomes Boltzmann's \(k_B\ln\Omega_m\) when all the probability lies in one macrocell. For a general ensemble it also contains uncertainty about which cell is occupied. Keeping these distinctions visible helps avoid making different entropies carry the same name without qualification.

The classical identities also leave open how to interpret the ensemble. We can regard \(\rho\) as a physical statistical state, or as the Hamiltonian transport of an agent's initial probabilities. The algebra compares that evolving density with a simpler reconstruction in either reading. It does not settle the interpretation of probability, or extend itself automatically into a quantum trajectory theorem.

I would still like to understand better when retaining an additional correlation improves prediction, and how the reconstruction gap relates to the memory kernel. A small gap between two observers certifies that those two resolutions agree closely; both may still miss substantial microscopic information. Predictive adequacy requires a dynamical calculation as well.

That is the question I want to keep working on. When information leaves our macroscopic record, where does it go, and on what timescale can it matter again? The entropy calculation tells us how much the chosen description has left out. The memory terms ask whether we can afford to leave it out. It is worth also thanking some people. In particular, Abhishek Yadav with whom, towards the end of this paper, started to collaborate on this project, found some inconsistencies that to me were worth fixing and would have warranted him co-authorship. He decided to decline for this one. We will work on some extensions, much more serious than this, in the future.
