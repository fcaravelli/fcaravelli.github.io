Koopman, Lotka-Volterra, and counting walks

After a few weeks of rapid fires on this blog, I had to take a break to work on something urgent at Planckian (stay tuned! also there combinatorics is involved). I want to discuss about something else. I recently gave a talk at one of the recurring Koopman meetings, which you can find here. The title was Two (non-standard) applications of the Koopman framework, and this already says a bit about what I wanted to do. The Koopman community is very active and quite large. Koopmanism was born in the 1930s, but it has had a renaissance in the last decade thanks to the work by Igor Mezic, and it is now a very active field of research.

Personally, I've been quite fashinated by Koopman operators for some time. A few years ago, when I arrived in Los Alamos, I had already asked about DMD (dynmic mode decomposition) to a colleague, and he gave me a code snippet written in Matlab to analyze some data. It made it to my Spectral Methods in Complex Systems notes, that I posted to the arXiv last year. As a matter of fact, Koopman operators nowadays are far from a theoretical device, but they are used in robotics and fluid flow analysis.

In fact, usually, when people say Koopman today, they often mean data-driven Koopmanism: dynamic mode decomposition, extended DMD, reduced models, control, fluids, and all the very useful technology that has grown from the idea that nonlinear dynamics can be studied through a linear operator acting on observables.

This is not what I wanted to discuss in the talk. I accidentally started to work on Koopman operators much later. The day after I explain this to Flavio Morone, who is a stat mech person working at NYU, he was quite fascinated by the fact that the Lotka-Volterra system was counting walks on a lattice.

The first part of the talk is pretty much about this, I will explain it here to some extent, and also I will mention the second part, which is about computation and reachability. The point of the first part was much stranger, and for this reason also fun if you like combinatorics: can one take a very classical nonlinear ODE, the Lotka-Volterra predator-prey system, and see inside it a problem about counting walks?

The point of the second part, which I will mention only briefly at the end, was instead: can one talk about computation, halting, reachability, and decidability using the same Koopman language?

The common thread is that nonlinear dynamics becomes a linear operator on observables. Once you do that, structures that look combinatorial, algorithmic, or even grammatical start to become spectral structures. I tell often this thing, that some time ago I stopped thinking about linear and nonlinear ODEs, and I started to think of them as finite or infinite linear systems. There is a huge difference between them!

Koopman in one paragraph

Suppose that we have a discrete dynamical system

$$ x_{t+1}=F(x_t). $$

The Koopman operator does not evolve the point \(x\). It evolves an observable \(g:X\to \mathbb{C}\):

$$ (Kg)(x)=g(F(x)). $$

The miracle, which is also the price you pay, is that \(K\) is linear even when \(F\) is nonlinear. The price is that \(K\) is usually infinite dimensional.

In continuous time, for

$$ \dot{x}=f(x), $$

the generator is

$$ L=f(x)\cdot \nabla. $$

So the Koopman equation for an observable \(\psi\) is

$$ \partial_t \psi = L\psi. $$

That is the entire magic trick. We replace nonlinear motion in phase space with linear motion in the space of functions. But of course, as usual in statistical mechanics, a simple formal trick becomes interesting only when you choose the right observables.

The Lotka-Volterra problem

The model I discussed in the first part of the talk was the two-species Lotka-Volterra system

$$ \dot{x}=\alpha x+\beta xy,\qquad \dot{y}=\gamma y+\delta xy, $$

with the ecological signs

$$ \alpha>0,\qquad \beta<0,\qquad \gamma<0,\qquad \delta>0. $$

Here \(x\) is the prey and \(y\) is the predator. The prey grows when left alone, the predator dies when left alone, and the nonlinear term \(xy\) is the encounter term. This is one of those equations that everybody has seen at some point. It is old, classical, and in some sense completely understood. But it is also not explicitly solvable in the elementary sense. You have a conserved quantity, yes, but not a nice \(x(t)\), \(y(t)\) formula that you can just write down and forget about.

The question we asked in the work with Yen Ting Lin was slightly perverse: if the equation is quadratic, does the quadratic structure hide a combinatorial object?

The answer is yes. In fact the answer is almost embarrassingly direct once you look at the right basis.

Carleman first: monomials become a lattice

Take the monomials

$$ r_{a,b}=x^a y^b. $$

Differentiate them along the flow:

$$ \begin{aligned} {d\over dt}x^a y^b &= a x^{a-1}\dot{x}y^b + b y^{b-1}\dot{y}x^a \\ &= (a\alpha+b\gamma)x^a y^b + a\beta x^a y^{b+1} + b\delta x^{a+1}y^b . \end{aligned} $$

In other words,

$$ \dot{r}_{a,b} = (a\alpha+b\gamma)r_{a,b} +a\beta r_{a,b+1} +b\delta r_{a+1,b}. $$

This is Carleman linearisation. The nonlinear ODE has become an infinite linear system. But the infinite matrix is not random. It is a directed weighted lattice.

From a site \((a,b)\), you can:

  • stay at \((a,b)\), with weight \(a\alpha+b\gamma\);
  • move right to \((a,b+1)\), with weight \(a\beta\);
  • move down to \((a+1,b)\), with weight \(b\delta\).

This is the point where the problem starts to look like statistical mechanics. The solution of the ODE is now hidden in the exponential of a weighted adjacency matrix. Expanding that exponential means summing over walks on this lattice.

One can almost hear the partition function trying to come out.

Then Mori-Zwanzig: all the nonlinearity sits in one observable

Now split the monomials into resolved and unresolved variables. The resolved variables are just \(x\) and \(y\). The unresolved variables are the nonlinear monomials, starting from \(xy\), \(x^2y\), \(xy^2\), and so on.

Applying the Mori-Zwanzig projection to this Carleman system gives an exact generalized Langevin equation. In this particular case, because the Carleman lattice only moves to higher degree, the memory kernel vanishes. There is no unresolved-to-resolved feedback in the linear system. Instead, all the nonlinear information appears as a deterministic forcing term.

That forcing term is controlled by a single scalar function, which I called \(\eta\) in the talk:

$$ \eta(s,x_0,y_0) = \sum_{c,d\geq 1} \left(e^{A_{uu}s}\right)_{(1,1),(c,d)} x_0^c y_0^d. $$

Then the solution can be written as

$$ x(t)=e^{\alpha t}x_0+ \beta e^{\alpha t}\int_0^t e^{-\alpha s}\eta(s,x_0,y_0)\,ds, $$

$$ y(t)=e^{\gamma t}y_0+ \delta e^{\gamma t}\int_0^t e^{-\gamma s}\eta(s,x_0,y_0)\,ds. $$

This is not yet a closed-form solution. But it is a very clean structural statement. The function \(\eta\) is the exponential generating function of all weighted directed walks starting from the corner \((1,1)\) of the lattice.

For instance, the first few coefficients are

$$ \Gamma_0=x_0y_0, $$

$$ \Gamma_1=\beta x_0y_0^2+\delta x_0^2y_0, $$

$$ \Gamma_2= \beta(2\alpha+3\gamma)x_0y_0^2 +\delta(3\alpha+2\gamma)x_0^2y_0 +4\beta\delta x_0^2y_0^2. $$

These coefficients are literally sums over allowed paths. The terms with \(\alpha\) and \(\gamma\) come from waiting on a site. The terms with \(\beta\) and \(\delta\) come from moving. The combinatorial complexity grows quickly, because at order \(k\) one has to sum over many possible orderings of stays, right moves, and down moves.

This also explains why the naive series is not a miracle numerical solver. It is a short-time expansion in disguise, and in the ecological sign convention some coefficients have alternating signs. So if the question is "did we solve Lotka-Volterra?", the honest answer is: no, not in the elementary formula sense.

But if the question is "what is the exact combinatorial skeleton of the nonlinear equation?", then the answer is very satisfying. I had derived most of this on my own, but I was still unhappy of what this was saying at all. I actually had not realized that the walk generator I had found could be written in terms of Koopman, and that is indeed the major contribution that Yen Ting noticed, and showed it also in the simpler case of the quadratic ode later on.

The punchline: the walk generator is the Koopman evolution of \(xy\)

The generating function \(\eta\) satisfies the PDE

$$ \partial_t \eta = (\alpha x+\beta xy)\partial_x\eta + (\gamma y+\delta xy)\partial_y\eta, \qquad \eta(0,x,y)=xy. $$

But the operator on the right-hand side is exactly the Koopman generator

$$ L=(\alpha x+\beta xy)\partial_x +(\gamma y+\delta xy)\partial_y. $$

Therefore

$$ \eta(t,x_0,y_0)=K_t(xy)(x_0,y_0). $$

And since Koopman evolution means "evaluate the observable along the trajectory", this is

$$ \eta(t,x_0,y_0)=x(t)y(t). $$

This is the part I like the most. The same object has three lives:

  • it is a generating function of weighted lattice walks;
  • it is the deterministic Mori-Zwanzig forcing term;
  • it is the Koopman-evolved observable \(xy\).

It is interesting because it connects otherwise disconnected languages, with one object. This is the sort of thing that makes me think that the Koopman formalism is still underused in statistical mechanics. Not because it magically solves nonlinear equations, but because it tells you what kind of object the nonlinearity is.

The context-free grammar connection

After working on this, I recently saw a beautiful paper by Shi-Mei Ma, Toufik Mansour, and David Callan from 2014, titled Some combinatorial arrays related to the Lotka-Volterra system. I somehow had missed it and is in fact not cited in our published paper. I'm quite surprised because I had sent the paper to Toufik, with whom I have published a paper in 2015.

Their language is not Koopman, and it is not Mori-Zwanzig. Their language is context-free grammars in the sense of Chen's grammatical method. This is an absolutely beautiful method developed in the early 90s, connecting context free grammars with the generator of polynomial families. But mathematically it is very close to the same story. In fact, I would now say that the only original contribution of the paper with YTL is the Koopman perspective.

In this method, one writes substitution rules such as

$$ G=\{x\to x+xy,\qquad y\to y+xy\}. $$

These rules define a formal derivative \(D\), by linearity and the Leibniz rule. Thus

$$ D(x)=x+xy,\qquad D(y)=y+xy, $$

and, for a monomial,

$$ D(x^i y^j) = (i+j)x^i y^j +i x^i y^{j+1} +j x^{i+1}y^j. $$

Compare this with the Carleman equation above. It is the same stay/right/down lattice, with the simple choice of unit weights. In vector-field language, this grammar is the differential operator

$$ D=x(1+y)\partial_x+y(1+x)\partial_y. $$

In other words, the grammar is the Koopman generator of the cooperative Lotka-Volterra-type system

$$ \dot{x}=x(1+y),\qquad \dot{y}=y(1+x). $$

This is not the ecological predator-prey sign convention, of course. But algebraically it is the same family. The grammar is a vector field, and repeated differentiation is the same as applying powers of the Koopman generator.

Ma, Mansour and Callan then write

$$ D^n(x)=\sum_{i\geq 1,j\geq 0}a_{n,i,j}x^i y^j. $$

The coefficients obey

$$ a_{n+1,i,j} = (i+j)a_{n,i,j} +i\,a_{n,i,j-1} +j\,a_{n,i-1,j}. $$

Again: stay, right, down.

They introduce the exponential generating function

$$ A(t,p,q)=\sum_{n,i,j\geq 0}a_{n,i,j}{t^n\over n!}p^i q^j, $$

and find that it satisfies

$$ A_t=p(1+q)A_p+q(1+p)A_q. $$

This is again a Koopman equation, just written in the variables of the generating function. They solve it and obtain

$$ A(t,p,q)= {p(p-q)e^t\over p-q\exp\!\left((p-q)(e^t-1)\right)}. $$

Even better, the coefficients have a clean combinatorial interpretation:

$$ a_{n,i,j} = \left\{ {n+1\atop i+j} \right\} \left\langle {i+j-1\atop i} \right\rangle, $$

where the first factor is a Stirling number of the second kind and the second is an Eulerian number. Equivalently, these numbers count cyclically ordered partitions of \([n+1]\) with \(i+j\) blocks, with a specified number of descents in the list of openers.

This is wonderfully strange. A predator-prey-looking differential operator is also a grammar. The grammar generates arrays of classical combinatorial numbers. Those arrays count ordered partitions with descent statistics. And the same recurrence is the recurrence for weighted walks on the Carleman lattice of the ODE.

They also study variants of the grammar, for example

$$ G=\{x\to x+xy,\qquad y\to y+x^2\}, $$

which is related to repeated differentiation of tangent and secant functions, and gives arrays involving peak and valley statistics. So this is not a one-off coincidence. The grammatical method is a machine for turning formal substitution rules into differential operators, and therefore into Koopman generators.

Said differently: in our paper, we started from the ODE and found walks. In their paper, they start from a grammar and find Lotka-Volterra. These are opposite directions through the same tunnel.

A statistical mechanics way to say it

If I had to summarize the first part of the talk in statistical-mechanics language, I would say the following.

A nonlinear ODE defines a transfer problem on observables. For polynomial nonlinearities, the monomial basis turns the Koopman generator into a weighted graph. The exponential of that generator is a sum over paths. The coefficients of the Taylor expansion are partition functions of walks with local weights.

In the Lotka-Volterra case the graph is a directed two-dimensional lattice. In an \(N\)-species Lotka-Volterra system it becomes a directed hyperlattice. The difficulty of the nonlinear problem is transferred into the enumeration, resummation, or simulation of walks on that hyperlattice.

This is why the connection to context-free grammars is so natural in retrospect. A grammar is also a local rule for producing descendants. A Carleman lattice is also a local rule for producing higher monomials. In both cases, iterating the rule creates a tree, or a family of walks, and the generating function obeys a first-order PDE.

There is a small philosophical point here which I like. We often think of ODEs as continuous objects and of combinatorics as discrete. But once you lift the ODE to observables, the distinction is much less clean. The ODE becomes an operator, the operator becomes a graph, and the graph wants to be counted.

The second part of the talk: computation

The second part of the talk was based on joint work with Jean-Charles Delvenne, and it was about computation through the Koopman framework. Actually, JC and I started to work on this almost by mistake: he gave a very nice talk about the reachability problem in dynamical systems, and I asked whether he had thought about the Koopman operator to phrase the problem. He had not. We met one year later at the Santa Fe Institute, and told me that "ah", by the way I thought about, and it is connected to the resolvent of the Koopman operator. I will write more in detail about this in other posts, but let me just say here what the connection to Koopman is.

The question there is: when does a dynamical system compute?

A Turing machine is a dynamical system. A cellular automaton is a dynamical system. A counter machine is a dynamical system. Even an analog computer is a dynamical system, although then one has to be much more careful about what is meant by precision and by halting.

The Koopman way to phrase reachability is through the resolvent. For \(|\lambda|>1\),

$$ (\lambda I-K)^{-1} = \lambda^{-1}\sum_{t\geq 0}\lambda^{-t}K^t. $$

If \(g_B\) is the characteristic function of a target set \(B\), then \(K^t g_B(x)\) asks whether the trajectory starting at \(x\) is in \(B\) at time \(t\). Therefore the resolvent is a generating function of the orbit.

Pair it with the characteristic function \(g_A\) of an initial set \(A\), and you get a Koopman version of the halting problem:

$$ g_A(\lambda I-K)^{-1}g_B\neq 0 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \text{some orbit from }A\text{ reaches }B. $$

Notice the echo with the Lotka-Volterra story. In the first part, the series counts walks on the Carleman lattice. In the second part, the series counts orbit segments from \(A\) to \(B\). The same operator-theoretic object is a generating function over paths.

One result we discussed is that if one step of the dynamics is computable, then the Koopman halting problem is recursively enumerable. If the system reaches the target, you eventually see it. If it never reaches the target, no finite simulation proves that it will never happen. This is the usual asymmetry of halting, but written as a statement about the resolvent of an operator.

Another point is that equicontinuous systems, meaning systems without sensitivity to initial conditions, cannot support universal computation in this sense. At finite resolution, the Krylov space generated by an observable is finite dimensional, so the reachability question collapses to finite linear algebra. No sensitivity, no unbounded computation.

Finally, in the digital direction, one can reverse the logic. If a physical system is engineered so that regions \(\Omega_q\) of its phase space represent machine states \(q\), then the characteristic functions \(\chi_q\) span a finite-dimensional Koopman-invariant subspace. On that subspace, the Koopman operator is just the transition matrix of the automaton. A halting basin is an eigenfunction with eigenvalue one.

This is a very clean dictionary:

  • machine states become observables;
  • transitions become the action of \(K\);
  • halting basins become \(\lambda=1\) eigenfunctions;
  • reachability becomes a resolvent question.

What I think is the larger point

What I like about this whole picture is not that Koopman theory suddenly solves everything. It does not. In the Lotka-Volterra case the PDE for \(\eta\) is basically as hard as the original ODE. In the computation case, the resolvent does not magically decide an undecidable problem.

The value is more structural.

Koopman gives a common language in which a nonlinear ODE, a context-free grammar, a lattice-walk generating function, and a halting problem all become statements about the evolution of observables. Sometimes the observable is \(xy\). Sometimes it is the characteristic function of a set. Sometimes it is a monomial in a grammar. But in each case the same idea is at work: lift the dynamics, linearize the action, and then ask what the operator is counting.

To me, this is the interesting part. There are places where statistical mechanics, combinatorics, and computation are not three separate subjects. They are three dialects of the same operator.

And every now and then, if you stare at a nonlinear equation long enough, it starts counting walks. In fact, I wonder whether one can use ODEs to model Monte Carlo problems. This is for another day.
