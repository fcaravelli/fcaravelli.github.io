The glorified scientist and the machine

July 26th, 2026

There are many conversations these days about the ability of LLMs to generate genuinely new results in Mathematics and Physics. I have seen a fair amount of panic in academic chats, on LinkedIn, at conferences and in private conversations.

I have been worried about this for roughly two years. What surprises me is not that scientists are worried now. What surprises me is how little urgency there was while the consequences appeared remote, and how quickly the discussion changed once academic employment, authorship and prestige seemed to be at stake.

We are often remarkably capable of turning a blind eye to people dying under bombs in a foreign country, while becoming suddenly alert when our own jobs may be threatened. Physicists are not necessarily intellectuals. Some are. Others merely perform the social role of the intellectual.

Yet beneath the panic lies a serious epistemological question: what does it mean to discover something when the key step is produced by a machine?

1. Who deserves credit?

Suppose someone writes a prompt and an AI system solves an open problem. Does the person who wrote the prompt deserve scientific credit?

The first answer is almost embarrassingly literal: credit was certainly required, although initially it was credit for tokens. This implies simply that money was somehow the source of the solution. The person who paid for the tokens may have been the one who wrote the prompt, but this creates an inequality (or rather, exhacerbates an existing one) between those who can afford to pay for tokens and those who cannot. The question of credit is therefore not only epistemological, but also social. But there is a more serious answer. A good question can be as important as its answer. Choosing the problem, recognizing that it is tractable, stating it correctly and understanding why it matters are all intellectual acts.

Two years ago, my good friend YTL told me in his office that he was beginning to place the arrival of large language models on the same historical plane as the atomic bomb. We had independently reached something close to the same conclusion.

At that time I was already impressed by the number of correct and useful things these systems could produce. But the human remained visibly inside the loop. One had to ask repeatedly, redirect the argument, repair errors and assemble fragments into a coherent result.

The difference today is not merely quantitative. In some cases the loop has become much shorter. A problem is stated once, a solution appears and a paper-like presentation follows. I have seen various people alerting the community lately. Even where the result is imperfect, the direction of travel is difficult to miss.

Around the time I effectively and physically left Los Alamos, I heard rumours that managers were already discussing whether scientists could eventually be replaced by AI. Replacing scientists wholesale would be a serious mistake. The more uncomfortable question is whether some institutions might attempt it anyway.

Universities face a related problem. Who learns when the solution is immediate? Who teaches? Who decides which problem is worth solving? What becomes of scientific training when the apprentice can summon an answer without acquiring the habits that once made the answer possible?

2. Knowledge without authorship

What follow is a familiar experience for me. You go to bed thinking about an equation. During the night something continues to work on it. In the morning the solution is suddenly present: an aha moment.

Did you derive the result?

It was produced by your brain, or by some part of your brain. But it was not produced by the conscious sequence of steps that you usually identify as yourself. The boundary between discovering a result and receiving it is already less clean than we often pretend.

Epistemology contains an even stranger version of the problem. If I ask God for the solution to an equation and God answers, can the answer count as knowledge? Oddly enough, the answer can be yes. Knowledge has traditionally been described, with important qualifications, as justified true belief.

The qualification matters. The Gettier problem shows that a belief may be true and apparently justified while still being true by accident (if continuously say it's 6 pm, at certain point you'll be right anyway). But the central point survives: knowledge is not normally defined by the biological identity of the entity from which the information arrived. But who verifies the result? Who checks that it is correct? Who understands why it is correct? Who can explain it to others? These were all human acts.

A proposed result must be true, or at least survive the strongest available attempts at verification. It must also be connected to reasons that justify accepting it. Whether the first suggestion came from a colleague, a dream, a divine revelation or a machine may be psychologically important without being epistemologically decisive.

3. Knowledge is not intelligence

The question of intelligence is more difficult.

Current language models are usually engaged through a task. We ask them to solve a particular equation, prove a statement, write a program or compare two ideas. Intelligence in the broader human sense involves something more mobile: the ability to enter a problem, leave it, carry a structure elsewhere and recognize that the same structure has reappeared in another disguise. This point of view is not new. It was already emphasized by Douglas Hofstadter in his 1979 book Gödel, Escher, Bach.

Douglas Hofstadter has long argued that analogy is not a decorative feature of thought but one of its central mechanisms. Even the acquisition of knowledge can be described in these terms. In predictive accounts of cognition, associated with Randall O’Reilly and many others before him, the brain continuously anticipates what comes next and adjusts its internal structure according to the discrepancy between prediction and observation.

Prediction itself is a form of analogy. We use previously acquired regularities to guess what the next state of the world will be. Learning occurs when the world refuses the analogy and forces us to modify it. We solve a problem when we recognize that a structure we have seen before is present in a new context. Otherwise we do a "random" search to find a solution.

Scientific intelligence therefore does more than answer. It selects, transfers, abandons, reframes and returns. It recognizes that a tool from one field may illuminate another. It may notice, for example, that an idea from parton physics can be connected to many-body localization. The analogy itself can be the important contribution, even before the calculation is complete.

4. Science, prestige and mimetic desire

Scientists often say that they care about results. I suspect that many care at least as much about the prestige attached to having their name associated with those results.

This is not necessarily a moral failure. Prestige is part of the incentive structure of science. It allocates jobs, grants, invitations, authority and attention. But it can gradually replace the purpose it was meant to reward.

René Girard’s idea of mimetic desire is useful here. We learn what to desire by observing what others desire. A community that visibly rewards recognition will train its younger members to seek recognition. The production of knowledge becomes the route to the object that appears socially valuable: prestige.

This creates a peculiar crisis when machines begin to produce the results. If the purpose of science is knowledge, then better machines are an extraordinary success. If the purpose is the glorification of scientists, then the same machines are an existential threat.

The question is therefore not only whether AI can do science. It is whether we do science to generate new knowledge or to become glorified scientists.

5. What happens to publishing?

There are, mathematically speaking, only three possibilities for the future volume of scientific publishing: more papers, the same number of papers or fewer papers.

My expectation is that we will first see a sharp increase. Producing a technically competent manuscript is becoming cheaper and faster. Once the bottleneck moves from calculation and writing to attention and verification, the number of submissions can rise much more quickly than the capacity of journals, referees or repositories to absorb them.

An exponential increase is not sustainable, especially for relatively small infrastructures such as arXiv. At some point the system will attempt to impose friction: limits on yearly submissions, stronger identity requirements, higher screening thresholds, proof certificates or restrictions tied to institutions.

I would be curious to see how large groups with fifty people and a principal investigator appearing on every paper respond to a strict personal submission quota.

After the flood may come contraction. If most incremental calculations can be generated automatically, then publishing every variation may lose its social value. The literature could become simultaneously larger, less readable and less important.

Personally, I stopped opening arXiv every morning around 2015. I still read papers, of course, but I no longer treat the daily list as a meaningful map of scientific reality. AI may simply make that mismatch impossible to ignore.

6. Why physics may be different

Mathematical research will probably feel the transformation before experimental physics. Physics remains, at its core, an experimental science: veritas per experimentum.

We may prove theorems, derive bounds and construct elegant formal structures, but when these activities detach completely from observation they become mathematical physics. Physics begins with the attempt to organize experimental regularities into a model, identify the effective variables and decide which idealizations are justified.

This does not make physics safe. Incremental work will be strongly affected first. If the problem is to extend an expansion, solve a known equation in a slightly different regime or apply an established method to a nearby model, or improve a bound, AI will increasingly be able to do much of the work.

I do not use incremental here as an insult. Most science is incremental, and it must be. Large conceptual changes are built from many smaller advances.

But deep work in a genuinely new area requires more than local optimization. It requires deciding that two distant bodies of knowledge belong together. It requires the movement in and out of formal systems that we call analogy, taste or physical intuition. AI may eventually transform this level as well. For now, it is the level at which the human contribution remains most visible.

The important word, however, is for now.

7. The process is part of the result

Science itself does not care whether a result was produced by a human, a dream, a revelation or an LLM. We care because we are human, and because for most of our history we were the principal agents in that cycle.

The analogy with climate change is imperfect but useful. The Earth does not care about global warming. It is a piece of rock moving through space. We care because the altered climate affects human and non-human life. I do not have air conditioning at home, so I care rather directly.

In the same way, science as an abstract process of producing valid descriptions does not care who performs the derivation. But we care about the experience of doing it.

I may be lucky enough to live another forty years. After that I will not care either way. What saddens me is the possibility that my son may never experience the peculiar joy of trying a thousand times to prove something, failing, carrying the problem for days and then suddenly seeing how it works.

Difficulty, trial and error are not merely obstacles placed before learning. They are part of learning. If solutions arrive too easily, we may lose not only the training produced by struggle but also the joy of trying.

There is a scene in the Italian film La profezia dell’armadillo in which Adriano Panatta reflects on the difference between playing correctly and creating beauty. To paraphrase the idea:

Some people play the game correctly; others create beauty. You can hear it in the sound of the ball. The greatest players do not merely hit it: they make it sing. Statistics cannot fully measure this, and words cannot explain it. Those who have experienced it recognize it immediately.

Physics has something similar. The process matters. A clean three-line proof can be better than a long and technically correct derivation. A good physical argument has rhythm, compression and inevitability. It does not merely arrive at the answer; it reveals why the answer had to be there. Many good scientist know the result before they write it down. The derivation is a performance, not a mere calculation.

Pace matters as well. I do not particularly enjoy prompting. Prompting feels unnatural as a way of doing science. It separates the production of an object from the continuous movement of thought.

I do use AI. I try, however, to limit how I use it. For example, I use Scribo to work from handwritten notes and then bring AI into the process. Writing by hand changes the pace. It keeps me inside the derivation rather than placing me outside it as a reader and inspector of generated results.

8. What comes after the glorified scientist?

AI-generated mathematics and theoretical work will still have to be checked. In that sense these systems resemble a one-way function: they may produce objects that are difficult to obtain but "relatively easy" to verify once a formal proof is available.

Systems such as Lean or PhysLean may therefore become increasingly important. The social unit of scientific work may shift from the individual derivation to a pipeline consisting of conjecture, machine generation, formal verification, experimental comparison and human interpretation.

Perhaps the figure of the glorified scientist will disappear. We may become something closer to modern farmers: operators of machines whose internal complexity vastly exceeds our individual capacity, responsible for choosing where to steer them, what to cultivate and what counts as a healthy result.

The machinery may consist of data centres, automated laboratories, theorem provers, robotic experimental platforms and models interacting with other models. The scientist may become less an isolated genius and more a driver, curator, critic and designer of questions.

We are living through a transformation comparable, perhaps, to the invention of agriculture, formal reason, industrial machinery or the atomic bomb. What emerges on the other side will be another equilibrium.

I remain uncertain whether it will be a better one.

What I learned from the transitions from Web 1.0 to Web 2.0 and then to the various promises of Web 3.0 is that the experience on the other side is often thinner than the one we imagined. More efficient, more scalable and less human.

I hope this time will be different.
