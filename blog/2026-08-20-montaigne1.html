de Montaigne, Peter Thiel, and the Red Queen effect

August 20th, 2026

This blog post is the convergence of various personal and not circumstances, readings, thoughts about the state of academia and science. It is the result of my inner paradoxes, e.g. my williness to compete versus trying to be more thoughtful. The math that follows is actually an attempt to make mathematical something I kept saying to people in words for some time.

I have been thinking about many of these things, and in particular recently after encountering, almost by accident, a few very different discussions. First, I was reading about the life of Michel de Montaigne. The algorithm then recommended Peter Thiel's famous Stanford lecture a few weeks back, Competition Is for Losers. Only afterward did I notice that the person introducing him was Sam Altman. Yuck. The talk is from 11 years ago.

The surprising thing (to me) is that de Montaigne and Thiel are, in a very loose sense, saying something similar: get out of the "rat race".

But they mean almost opposite things by it. de Montaigne wants to recover a life that is not exhausted by status and public performance. Thiel wants to stop competing on dimensions chosen by everybody else and occupy a position in which comparison itself becomes difficult. One retreats from the game; the other changes the game.

While writing this post I also watched this conversation with Samo Burja about why science may have slowed down . I do not agree with every claim in it, but it sharpened the question I was already asking: what is genuinely good about science as a human activity, and which parts of the modern scientific machinery are merely proxies that have slowly become confused with science itself?

The current AI boom makes the question more urgent because it is producing a new kind of rat race. AI can increase what an individual scientist is able to do. But if everybody receives the same kind of acceleration, the equilibrium response may simply be that everybody is expected to run faster.

1. de Montaigne decided to leave the race

I have been reading about de Montaigne because I saw a few analogies with myself. Michel de Montaigne was born in 1533 into a wealthy family near Bordeaux (unlike mine). He became a magistrate and eventually served in the Parlement of Bordeaux. These were not marginal positions in sixteenth-century France. They belonged to precisely the type world of status, office, political connections, reputation, and public advancement that ambitious people were expected to pursue.

Then, in 1571, around the age of thirty-eight, de Montaigne withdrew from his judicial career. (Funnily enough, this is roughly the same age at which I decided to quit my LANL job.)

He returned to his family estate, made the tower library his intellectual headquarters, and began writing what became the Essais. The word essai in French means an attempt, a trial, an experiment. Montaigne was not constructing a philosophical system in the manner of someone trying to establish the final architecture of reality. He was trying things out on himself.

His subject was, famously, de Montaigne himself, e.g. he became pensive, introspective - maybe as a form of healing, I would add, but that is beyond the point. In this sense he can feel like a proto-psychologist, a proto-anthropologist, and a proto-sociologist, well before any of those fields existed. He tried to understand the human condition by observing one particularly convenient specimen: himself.

This can sound narcissistic until one reads him. What makes de Montaigne important is almost the opposite. He distrusts grandiosity. He distrusts certainty. He distrusts the strange human tendency to turn one's position in a social hierarchy into the measure of one's existence.

His famous question was Que sais-je?: what do I know?

This was written in a century saturated with people who were quite certain that they knew. Europe was being torn apart by political and religious conflict. de Montaigne's response was not indifference, but epistemic modesty. Human beings are fallible, embodied, inconsistent creatures. Wisdom begins partly by noticing this.

What I find especially attractive is his idea of the arrière-boutique, literally the back room behind the shop: an interior place that does not belong completely to one's profession, public identity, obligations, family role, or status. We should reserve some part of ourselves that cannot be fully colonized by the world outside. AI makes me feel that, right now, a form of arrière-boutiqueism is needed.

His retirement should not be romanticized as permanent disappearance from public life. de Montaigne later travelled and served two terms as mayor of Bordeaux. The important point is that he created a distance between the "office" and the "self". He could return to public life without allowing public life to become the entirety of his identity.

There is a useful contrast here with modernity. The scientific revolution did not put humans at the cosmological center of the universe; in an important sense it displaced us from it. The industrial revolution, however, enormously increased our practical power over the world. AI does something stranger still, to which I will come back later: it expands individual cognitive reach while simultaneously making the individual human contribution feel less central.

Interistingly, also Dostoevsky explores a related theme in Crime and Punishment. One line that has stayed with me is the accusation that the worst sin is to have "destroyed and betrayed yourself for nothing." The point is not that one should never compromise. It is that there is something particularly empty about sacrificing oneself to a game one did not even choose.

Near the end of his life, de Montaigne's ideal was wonderfully unheroic: he wanted death to find him planting cabbages, unconcerned with death and even more unconcerned with the unfinished state of his garden. I recently bought a piece of land with something like that idea in mind. How many parallels.

There is something almost offensive about this attitude to modern professional culture. The garden is unfinished. The work is unfinished. The CV is unfinished. The career is unfinished. This refusal to require completion before life can count as successful is almost a small rebellion.

2. Peter Thiel also leaves the competition

The discussion above is not meant as a historical comparison but an introduction to what follows. de Montaigne was a sixteenth-century French essayist. If you don't happen to know him, Peter Thiel is instead an investor and entrepreneur. Famous for the so called PayPal mafia at the beginning of his career (including Musk, who had there a more marginal role than often assumed). One may dislike Thiel's politics, his style, or the social world he represents. I am not trying to make you like Peter Thiel, but I am trying to use his point of view here for my purpose.

His claim that "competition is for losers" is usually read as a statement about monopoly. At the business level, that is exactly what it is. A company operating in near-perfect competition has little ability to capture the value it creates. A company that does something genuinely unique can occupy a market in which direct comparison becomes much less meaningful.

But the more interesting part of the lecture comes near the end, when Thiel stops talking about firms and starts talking about people.

Competition, he argues, is psychologically attractive because it validates us. Let me try to make use an academic analogy. If many intelligent people are trying to enter the same profession, win the same fellowship, publish in the same journal, enter the same university, or solve the same fashionable problem, then pursuing it feels important. The crowd supplies the proof.

In business, Thiel recommends starting with a market so narrow that a company can dominate it and only later expanding outward. The scientific analogue is easy to see. One can become the person associated with a small problem, a small method, a small community, and then expand the circle. "I am the only theoretical memristive-device person in Pisa" is not a business plan, but it has the same type of vibe to what Thiel describes. He used the example of the only "British restaurant in Palo Alto".

What struck me especially was his discussion of scientists. Scientists, he suggests, are often motivated less by money than by recognition from their peers. From one point of view that is admirable. Science would be much worse if money were the only motive. But a desire for peer recognition is also exploitable. Prestige can become a currency even when nobody calls it one.

Thiel's objection is that human beings are intensely mimetic. He uses the idea in a Girardian sense; René Girard was one of his teachers at Stanford. We look around, discover what other ambitious people desire, and begin desiring it ourselves. Eventually we become extremely good at climbing ladders without remembering why that particular wall deserved a ladder.

He tells the story of leaving a prestigious New York law firm after seven months and three days. A colleague told him that seeing Thiel leave was reassuring because he had not realized escape was possible. The joke is that escape required nothing more complicated than walking through the front door and not returning.

de Montaigne and Thiel are therefore saying superficially similar things for very different purposes. Instead, de Montaigne says: stop needing to win. Preserve a part of yourself that the game cannot price. Thiel says: stop playing games in which winning means doing slightly better than everybody standing next to you.

de Montaigne withdraws from competition because life is finite. Thiel withdraws from competition because competition produces sameness. Conventional accounts of capitalism celebrate competition; Thiel's Silicon Valley strategy is precisely to escape it by building something monopolistic enough that nobody else is in the room.

One wants to leave competition to enjoy life. The other wants to avoid competition to take the market. In that sense, Thiel is a more ruthless de Montaigne.

3. AI moves the scientific distribution

These two perspectives matter because they offer two different responses to the way AI is entering science. The Montaignean response is to protect some part of one's intellectual life from the race. The Thielian response is to search for problems on which one is not forced into direct competition with a thousand nearly identical researchers. In both cases, however, it is important to understand the meaning or the context of the world "competing".

Whatever one thinks about the broader effects of AI on humankind, it has already changed the effective level at which an individual researcher can operate. One can move much more quickly through an unfamiliar literature. Symbolic calculations that once consumed an afternoon can be checked in minutes. Code can be written, translated, debugged, and explained rapidly. One can interrogate methods from a neighboring field without first spending six months becoming locally fluent in its dialect.

The natural picture is that the distribution of effective scientific capability moves to the right, where the horizontal axis now is capability. That sounds unambiguously good, and locally it often is.

But there is a new equilibrium problem hiding inside the obvious productivity gain.

Science is not carried out by one isolated researcher receiving a better tool while everyone else remains fixed. Everyone receives the tool, although not equally and not at exactly the same time. Authors adapt. Referees adapt. Students adapt. Journals adapt. Standards adapt.

So the relevant question is not merely:

$$\text{How much more can I do with AI?}$$

It is also:

$$\text{What happens when everybody can do more?}$$

This is where the problem starts to look like a small statistical-mechanical model. The model below is deliberately a toy model. It is not a measurement of scientific talent or a claim that citations are equivalent to scientific value. Its purpose is to isolate an incentive mechanism.

4. A Gaussian model of scientific accessibility

Let me use a simple model to illustrate the problem. It also applies, in a weaker form, to the scientific publication industry, before the existence of tools such as modern AI.

Let me try to explain this. We assign to every scientist an effective field-specific sophistication level $X$. This is not IQ. It is a compressed proxy containing various generic abilities, such as mathematical preparation, familiarity with the literature, technical experience, conceptual background, judgment, and the other ingredients required to absorb a particular piece of work.

Suppose, for simplicity, that this level is distributed according to Gaussian, or the spherical-cow approximation:

$$X\sim\mathcal N(\mu,\sigma^2).$$

Now assign to any a paper a certain level of technical depth $d$. Imagine first a sharp absorption threshold: a researcher can productively use the paper only when

$$X\geq d.$$

This is obviously an idealization. Understanding is not binary, and different papers demand different kinds of expertise. But the threshold makes the mechanism transparent.

Let

$$z=\frac{d-\mu}{\sigma}$$

denote how many standard deviations the paper sits above the mean. The fraction of the scientific population capable of absorbing the paper is then

$$A(d)=1-\Phi(z) =1-\Phi\!\left(\frac{d-\mu}{\sigma}\right).$$

We now introduce another quantity, $q(d)$, denoting the intrinsic scientific value of a contribution of depth $d$, conditional on somebody actually being able to understand and use it. If there are $N$ potential readers, a deliberately crude model of realized recognition is

$$C(d)=Nq(d) \left[ 1-\Phi\!\left(\frac{d-\mu}{\sigma}\right) \right].$$

The formula says mathematically something much simpler, i.e. that there are two competing effects. First, increasing $d$ may increase the scientific value of the work. But it simultaneously moves the paper farther into the upper tail of the population capable of reading it.

I often tell colleagues some version of the following: a very good paper that nobody can absorb may be scientifically valuable, but it is badly positioned for recognition (or citations). That does not mean that the optimal paper must sit exactly at the peak of the sophistication distribution. The optimum depends on how quickly scientific value grows with depth relative to how quickly accessibility disappears. In the model above, this can be seen using the following manipulation.

We take logarithms on both sides,

$$\log C(d) = \log N+\log q(d) +\log\left[1-\Phi(z)\right].$$

and differentiating with respect to $d$, we obtain

$$ \frac{\partial}{\partial d}\log C(d) = \frac{q'(d)}{q(d)} - \frac{1}{\sigma} \frac{\phi(z)}{1-\Phi(z)}. $$

An important quantity that we will see entering the narrative is the inverse Mills ratio,

$$ m(z)=\frac{\phi(z)}{1-\Phi(z)}. $$

Then

$$ \frac{\partial}{\partial d}\log C(d) = \frac{q'(d)}{q(d)} - \frac{1}{\sigma}m(z). $$

This equation contains a picture of what I am try to describe. The first term is the proportional gain from moving deeper into the scientific frontier. The second is the accessibility penalty: how quickly the potential population of readers disappears as the paper becomes more demanding.

For simplicity, take the particularly simple form

$$q(d)=e^{\gamma d},\qquad \gamma>0.$$

Then

$$\frac{q'(d)}{q(d)}=\gamma,$$

and the recognition-maximizing sophistication $d^*$ satisfies

$$ m(z^*)=\gamma\sigma. $$

Therefore, one obtains the equation for the maximum below,

$$ { d^* = \mu+\sigma m^{-1}(\gamma\sigma) }. $$

The expression above, should be taken with care. There is no universal rule saying that the optimal paper sits one standard deviation above the mean scientist. However, this is what I kept telling people in words, and now I'm using a formal argument for it. Nonetheless, the location depends on the scientific return to depth, $\gamma$, and on the width of the sophistication distribution, $\sigma$.

The model gives a distinction that is often lost when we talk casually about "better science." Scientific quality and scientific recognition are not the same object. Recognition is quality filtered through a population capable of absorbing the work. Tails may be for the brave rather than the careerist. The deeper question is what we do science for. It also serves as a good baseline for the argument that I would like to make below, which is the red queen effect when AI is added to the picture.

5. A Red Queen effect

We now try to repeat the same argument as before, while introducing a tool that improves the capability of the single researcher, i.e. we introduce AI. The simplest possible assumption is that AI increases the effective sophistication of every scientist by a fixed amount $a$ (and this amount depends on the model generation):

$$X^{AI}=X+a.$$

Under this assumption the distribution shifts to the right,

$$X^{AI}\sim\mathcal N(\mu+a,\sigma^2),$$

but its width does not change. This detail will matter later: the toy model says nothing by itself about whether AI makes real scientific capability more equal or more unequal.

A paper of depth $d$ can now be absorbed when

$$X+a\geq d,$$

or equivalently

$$X\geq d-a.$$

Following the same analysis as before, the accessible fraction becomes

$$ A(d;a) = 1-\Phi\!\left( \frac{d-a-\mu}{\sigma} \right). $$

With $q(d)=e^{\gamma d}$,

$$ C(d;a) = N e^{\gamma d} \left[ 1-\Phi\!\left( \frac{d-a-\mu}{\sigma} \right) \right]. $$

We now define

$$ z=\frac{d-a-\mu}{\sigma}. $$

and repeating the derivative again, we obtain

$$ \frac{\partial}{\partial d}\log C = \gamma-\frac{1}{\sigma}m(z). $$

The optimum therefore obeys the same standardized condition,

$$m(z^*)=\gamma\sigma.$$

Hence $z^*$ is constant and

$$ { d^*(a) = a+\mu+\sigma m^{-1}(\gamma\sigma) }. $$

Consequently,

$$ { \frac{\partial d^*}{\partial a}=1 }. $$

This is the result I find both beautiful, but slightly disturbing.

If AI gives everyone one additional unit of effective scientific capability, the recognition-maximizing paper becomes one unit more sophisticated.

The relevant gap is

$$d^*(a)-a,$$

and

$$ d^*(a)-a = \mu+\sigma m^{-1}(\gamma\sigma), $$

which does not depend on $a$. Therefore the accessible fraction at the moving optimum is also unchanged:

$$ A(d^*(a);a) = 1-\Phi\!\left[m^{-1}(\gamma\sigma)\right]. $$

In words, everybody becomes more capable. But the endogenous frontier moves by exactly the same amount. Nobody becomes relatively closer to it. There is no "we will now publish PRLs". PRL will adapt to accept papers shifted in quality by the AI. Consequently, who does things "old style", by hand, will necessarily be worse off. For the italian readership, this might be translated also as "la corsa sul posto", which is often used by the journalist de Il Post, Francesco Costa.

This is a scientific Red Queen effect: we run faster in order to remain in the same place. In biology, more precisely, the red queen effect or hypothesis, is that species must constantly adapt and evolve, just to remain in the same spot in relation to the other species, see for instance the Wikipedia place. The effect is inspired by the Red Queen in Lewis Carroll story, Through the looking glass: "Now, here, you see, it takes all the running you can do, to keep in the same place.".

Notice what did not happen. The model does not say AI is useless. At any fixed paper depth $d$, raising $a$ makes the paper more accessible. The disappearance of that gain occurs only because scientific production responds endogenously and moves $d$ as well.

The mistake would be to hold the frontier fixed while changing the capabilities of the people competing near it.

6. A tragedy of the scientific commons

The previous section described a Red Queen effect, but there was actually no competition between scientists in the mathematical model. AI gave everybody the same additional capability $a$, and the optimal depth of scientific work moved by exactly the same amount. Everybody became more capable, but the frontier moved with them. Relative to the frontier, nobody had gained any ground.

I now want to change the problem in one important way.

Until now, the depth $d$ of a paper was chosen as if that paper existed alone. But papers do not exist alone. They compete with other papers for readers, citations, invitations, journal space, grants, jobs, and, more generally, for the attention of the scientific community.

This introduces a genuinely strategic element into the problem. The best choice for me can now depend on what everybody else is doing.

The reason is that science contains a resource that is genuinely scarce: attention. Knowledge itself is not rival or competitive. My understanding of an equation does not prevent you from understanding it. Attention is different: if I am paying attention to paper A, then I am paying less attention to paper B. An hour spent reading one paper cannot simultaneously be spent reading another one. There are only so many papers one can read, talks one can attend, manuscripts one can referee, grant proposals one can evaluate, and ideas one can seriously think about.

This is the sense in which I want to use the expression tragedy of the commons. The classical reference is, of course, Garrett Hardin's 1968 essay The Tragedy of the Commons . In complexity science and in particular economics it is a quite important paper. Hardin considered situations in which individuals obtain a private benefit by making use of a shared finite resource, while part of the cost of that decision is imposed on everybody else.

The analogy here is of course not literal. Scientists are not grazing cattle on the same field (here meaning the one with grass!). The common resource is the finite cognitive bandwidth of the scientific community. What one paper captures in attention is attention that is not available, at that moment, to another paper.

And, importantly, a commons does not automatically imply tragedy. Elinor Ostrom's work on the governance of common resources showed precisely that communities can develop institutions that prevent individually sensible decisions from producing collectively bad outcomes. So what follows is not an argument that science must end up in this equilibrium. I only want to show that the incentive exists.

Let us now put this idea into the model.

Suppose there are $M$ papers (for simplicity on the same topic). This is not a stupid example. A few weeks ago, four papers appeared on the same open problem, all claiming the use of AI. Now, paper $i$ has scientific depth $d_i$. I will assume that every paper receives some fraction $v_i$ of the total available scientific attention, with

$$ v_i = \frac{e^{\rho(a)d_i}} {\sum_{j=1}^{M}e^{\rho(a)d_j}}. $$

This is just a convenient way of describing a competition for attention. The important property is

$$ \sum_{i=1}^{M}v_i=1. $$

There is one unit of attention to distribute. A paper can increase its share, but the total amount being divided does not change.

The parameter $\rho(a)$ tells us how strongly attention responds to scientific depth. If $\rho$ is small, being slightly more technically sophisticated than everybody else does not buy much additional attention. If $\rho$ is large, small differences in apparent frontier sophistication can strongly affect which work gets noticed.

I have written $\rho(a)$ rather than simply $\rho$ because later I want to ask whether AI itself can intensify this competition. But that will be an additional assumption. For the moment we can simply regard $a$ as fixed.

The realized impact of paper $i$ is now

$$ Y_i = N e^{\gamma d_i} \left[ 1-\Phi\!\left( \frac{d_i-a-\mu}{\sigma} \right) \right]v_i. $$

There are now three distinct pieces in this expression.

The factor $e^{\gamma d_i}$ is the same benefit from scientific depth that we introduced before: deeper work can potentially contain more scientific value.

The factor

$$ 1-\Phi\!\left( \frac{d_i-a-\mu}{\sigma} \right) $$

is the accessibility penalty. If the paper becomes too demanding relative to the effective sophistication of the population, fewer people can absorb it.

Finally, $v_i$ is the new ingredient. It represents the fraction of collective attention captured by this particular paper.

We can now ask what an individual scientist will do.

Imagine that I am the author of paper $i$. Everybody else has already chosen the depth of their papers,

$$ d_1,\ldots,d_{i-1},d_{i+1},\ldots,d_M. $$

I take those choices as given and choose my own $d_i$ in order to maximize my own realized impact $Y_i$. This is the elementary strategic problem underlying what is called a Nash equilibrium.

A Nash equilibrium is simply a configuration in which, once everybody else's choices are fixed, no individual can improve their own outcome by changing their choice alone.

In our case, an equilibrium depth is therefore one for which no author wants to make their own paper slightly deeper or slightly shallower while the other papers remain unchanged.

Let us derive this condition rather than simply state it.

It is easier to differentiate the logarithm of $Y_i$. Since

$$ \log Y_i = \log N + \gamma d_i + \log\left[ 1-\Phi\!\left( \frac{d_i-a-\mu}{\sigma} \right) \right] + \log v_i, $$

we can examine the three contributions separately.

First,

$$ \frac{\partial}{\partial d_i}(\gamma d_i)=\gamma. $$

This is the direct reward for going deeper.

Second, defining

$$ z_i=\frac{d_i-a-\mu}{\sigma}, $$

the derivative of the accessibility term is

$$ \frac{\partial}{\partial d_i} \log[1-\Phi(z_i)] = -\frac{1}{\sigma} \frac{\phi(z_i)}{1-\Phi(z_i)} = -\frac{1}{\sigma}m(z_i). $$

This is the cost of going deeper: as $d_i$ rises, the paper moves farther into the upper tail of what the scientific population can comfortably absorb.

Finally, we have the new attention term. Taking the logarithm of $v_i$ gives

$$ \log v_i = \rho(a)d_i - \log\left( \sum_{j=1}^{M}e^{\rho(a)d_j} \right). $$

Differentiating with respect to my own $d_i$, while keeping everybody else's depth fixed, gives

$$ \frac{\partial\log v_i}{\partial d_i} = \rho(a)-\rho(a)v_i = \rho(a)(1-v_i). $$

This term has an intuitive meaning. Making my paper deeper can increase my share of attention. The gain is positive as long as I do not already possess all the attention, that is, as long as $v_i\le 1$.

Putting the three pieces together,

$$ \frac{\partial\log Y_i}{\partial d_i} = \underbrace{\gamma}_{\text{scientific value}} - \underbrace{\frac{1}{\sigma}m(z_i)}_{\text{loss of accessibility}} + \underbrace{\rho(a)(1-v_i)}_{\text{gain in attention}}. $$

The individual optimum occurs when these effects balance:

$$ \gamma - \frac{1}{\sigma} m\!\left( \frac{d_i-a-\mu}{\sigma} \right) + \rho(a)(1-v_i) = 0. $$

This equation is worth reading in words. I make the paper deeper until the marginal loss of readers is exactly compensated by two benefits: the additional scientific value of depth and the additional share of attention I can capture from other papers.

We now look for the simplest possible Nash equilibrium: a symmetric one. This means that all authors face the same incentives and eventually choose the same depth,

$$ d_1=d_2=\cdots=d_M=d_N. $$

If all papers have the same depth, symmetry immediately implies that they divide attention equally:

$$ v_i=\frac{1}{M}. $$

It is useful to collect the competitive term into a single quantity,

$$ R(a) = \rho(a)\left(1-\frac{1}{M}\right). $$

The equilibrium condition then becomes

$$ \frac{1}{\sigma} m\!\left( \frac{d_N-a-\mu}{\sigma} \right) = \gamma+R(a). $$

Defining the standardized equilibrium depth

$$ z_N = \frac{d_N-a-\mu}{\sigma}, $$

we have

$$ m(z_N)=\sigma[\gamma+R(a)], $$

and therefore

$$ d_N(a) = a+\mu+ \sigma m^{-1}\!\left( \sigma[\gamma+R(a)] \right). $$

So far we have found what individually optimizing scientists will do. But this is not yet enough to speak of a tragedy. We need something to compare the Nash equilibrium with. We need to ask what would happen if the scientific community could somehow choose collectively rather than competitively.

Imagine, as a thought experiment, that all authors agreed to change the depth of their papers together. If everybody increases $d_i$ by the same amount, nobody gains attention relative to anybody else. In the symmetric case,

$$ v_i=\frac{1}{M} $$

remains exactly unchanged.

We can see this directly by adding the realized impacts of all papers. In a symmetric configuration, where every paper has depth $d$,

$$ \sum_{i=1}^{M}Y_i = N e^{\gamma d} \left[ 1-\Phi\!\left( \frac{d-a-\mu}{\sigma} \right) \right] \sum_{i=1}^{M}v_i. $$

But the attention shares sum to one. Therefore

$$ \sum_{i=1}^{M}Y_i = N e^{\gamma d} \left[ 1-\Phi\!\left( \frac{d-a-\mu}{\sigma} \right) \right]. $$

The attention competition disappears completely from the collective objective. Redistributing attention between papers cannot create more total attention.

The collectively chosen depth therefore satisfies exactly the same condition we found before introducing competition:

$$ m(z_C)=\gamma\sigma, $$

and hence

$$ d_C(a) = a+\mu+\sigma m^{-1}(\gamma\sigma). $$

Now we can finally compare the two outcomes.

In the collective solution, scientific depth is increased only when its scientific benefit compensates for the loss of accessibility. In the Nash equilibrium, every scientist has one additional reason to increase depth: doing so may steal some attention from everybody else.

Since $m(z)$ is increasing, $m^{-1}$ is increasing as well. Therefore, whenever there is positive competition for attention,

$$ R(a)>0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad d_N(a)>d_C(a). $$

This is the central result.

Individually rational competition pushes scientific work beyond the depth that would be chosen collectively.

Each scientist has an incentive to make their work more sophisticated because, taken alone, this can increase their share of attention. But once everybody responds in the same way, the relative advantage disappears. Everyone again has roughly the same share, while the additional complexity remains.

That is the sense in which this resembles a tragedy of the commons. The private gain is positional, while part of the cost is imposed on the community through a literature that becomes more expensive to read, evaluate, reproduce, and absorb.

Notice that this is already different from the Red Queen effect of the previous section. There, the frontier simply moved together with our capabilities. Here there is a wedge between two quantities:

$$ d_N(a)-d_C(a)>0. $$

The decentralized scientific system produces more equilibrium complexity than the coordinated system would choose.

We can now ask one final question. So far, we have compared the Nash and collective equilibria for a fixed level of AI capability $a$. But what happens to the Nash equilibrium itself as AI becomes more powerful?

There are actually two possible effects.

The first is the one we already know. Increasing $a$ directly increases everyone's effective capability. Even with no change in competition, this shifts equilibrium depth to the right. That was the Red Queen effect.

The second possibility is new. AI might also make the competition for attention stronger. If producing calculations, simulations, code, figures, literature reviews, and polished papers becomes cheaper, more material can compete for the same finite human attention. In our model, that possibility is represented by

$$ R'(a)>0. $$

This is an additional assumption, not a mathematical consequence of AI. What I want to understand is what follows if it happens.

Recall that the Nash equilibrium is determined by

$$ m(z_N)=\sigma[\gamma+R(a)]. $$

We now want to know how $z_N$ changes when $a$ changes. In other words, we differentiate the equilibrium condition itself with respect to $a$.

The left-hand side depends on $a$ through $z_N$, so the chain rule gives

$$ m'(z_N)\frac{\mathrm d z_N}{\mathrm d a}. $$

On the right-hand side, $\gamma$ and $\sigma$ are constant, while $R$ may depend on $a$. Therefore

$$ m'(z_N)\frac{\mathrm d z_N}{\mathrm d a} = \sigma R'(a). $$

Solving for the change in the standardized gap gives

$$ \frac{\mathrm d z_N}{\mathrm d a} = \frac{\sigma R'(a)}{m'(z_N)}. $$

Now remember what $z_N$ means:

$$ z_N = \frac{d_N-a-\mu}{\sigma}. $$

Equivalently,

$$ d_N=a+\mu+\sigma z_N. $$

Differentiating this expression is particularly illuminating:

$$ \frac{\mathrm d d_N}{\mathrm d a} = 1 + \sigma\frac{\mathrm d z_N}{\mathrm d a}. $$

Substituting the expression above,

$$ { \frac{\mathrm d d_N}{\mathrm d a} = 1+ \frac{\sigma^2R'(a)}{m'(z_N)} }. $$

This formula is much easier to interpret than it might initially appear.

The first term,

$$1,$$

is precisely the Red Queen effect from the previous section. If AI gives scientists one additional unit of capability and nothing else changes, equilibrium scientific depth increases by one unit.

The second term,

$$ \frac{\sigma^2R'(a)}{m'(z_N)}, $$

is the new strategic effect. It measures how much additional depth is produced because AI has also changed the intensity of competition for attention.

For the Gaussian inverse Mills ratio,

$$ m'(z)=m(z)[m(z)-z]>0. $$

Therefore, if stronger AI also means stronger competition,

$$ R'(a)>0, $$

then the second term is positive and

$$ { \frac{\mathrm d d_N}{\mathrm d a}>1 }. $$

This is stronger than the Red Queen effect.

In the previous section, one additional unit of scientific capability generated one additional unit of optimal depth. Here, one additional unit of capability can generate more than one unit of equilibrium depth, because the technological improvement is accompanied by a stronger positional competition.

The frontier is no longer simply moving with us. It is moving faster than us.

We can make that statement precise by looking again at $z_N$. This quantity measures the depth of the equilibrium literature relative to the AI-augmented capability of the scientific population:

$$ z_N= \frac{d_N-a-\mu}{\sigma}. $$

If $R'(a)>0$, then

$$ \frac{\mathrm d z_N}{\mathrm d a}>0. $$

In other words, even after subtracting the direct capability gain $a$, the equilibrium literature moves farther into the upper tail of the population.

The fraction of scientists capable of absorbing a paper at the equilibrium depth is

$$ A_N(a)=1-\Phi(z_N). $$

Differentiating,

$$ \frac{\mathrm d A_N}{\mathrm d a} = -\phi(z_N) \frac{\mathrm d z_N}{\mathrm d a}. $$

Since $\phi(z_N)>0$, whenever $R'(a)>0$ we obtain

$$ { \frac{\mathrm d A_N}{\mathrm d a}\le 0 }. $$

And this is the stronger paradox I was looking for:

AI can make every scientist individually more capable while the competitive equilibrium makes the scientific literature relatively less accessible.

It is important to be precise about what the model is saying. AI itself is not making science less accessible. The direct effect of AI is to increase capability. The deterioration appears only if the scientific system responds to that capability by intensifying a positional competition over scarce attention.

An individual scientist may therefore have every reason to add another derivation, another simulation, another model, another benchmark, another appendix, another layer of formal machinery. If one scientist does it, that scientist may gain an advantage. If everybody does it, the relative advantage largely disappears. But the extra machinery remains.

This is precisely where Thiel re-enters the story. Competition can make us extraordinarily good at whatever dimension has become the basis of competition. It cannot tell us whether that dimension was worth optimizing in the first place.

And AI may make this particularly important because it lowers the cost of participating in the race. If everybody can produce more derivations, more code, more simulations, more literature review, more polished prose, and more papers, the equilibrium response need not be more leisure, more reflection, or more time spent thinking about difficult questions.

It may simply be that the expected quantity of all those things increases.

A few posts ago I speculated that AI might flatten some differences in scientific capability. This toy model cannot establish that. By construction, adding the same $a$ to everybody shifts the mean of the capability distribution and leaves $\sigma$ unchanged. Unequal access to models, compute, data, institutional support, reputation, or simply unequal ability to use AI could instead change the shape of the distribution in the real world. But that is another mechanism, and ultimately an empirical question. It does not follow from the equations above.

7. Science is not (simply) a KPI

This is where the Samo Burja conversation I mentioned at the beginning became useful to this discussion. I talked before about Vannevar Bush The scientific frontier, and which is mentioned in the conversation above.

Burja's claim is that science may have slowed down, and that the explanation is not simply that humanity picked all the low-hanging fruit. He distinguishes scientific progress from technological progress: making a machine larger, faster, or cheaper can be a genuine technological achievement without necessarily giving us a new theory of why nature works the way it does.

I am not convinced by every example, and "science has slowed down" is much too broad a sentence to be accepted without qualifications. Different fields behave differently. Instrumentation matters. Large collaborations can discover things that solitary geniuses simply cannot. Modern peer review, grant systems, and institutions also exist for reasons: science at scale needs error correction, accountability, continuity, and ways of distributing scarce resources.

But one part of the discussion struck me. Burja argues that science depends on a loop between theory and empirical observation, and that some of the most important scientific abilities are difficult to transmit as formal instructions. A textbook can teach you the result. It is much harder for it to teach you what he calls the "generators": the habits of mind that let somebody invent the question, choose the right approximation, notice the anomaly, or decide which ugly calculation is worth doing.

This is tacit knowledge. It is one reason mentorship matters. It is also one reason the separation between theoreticians and experimentalists can occasionally become unhealthy: the person who understands the theory may not be close enough to the apparatus to notice what the apparatus is trying to say, while the person running the apparatus may be too far from the theory to know which tiny deviation is conceptually explosive.

There is something very nice about science when this loop works. Science is one of the few human activities in which being wrong can be productive, in which a sufficiently stubborn fact can humiliate a beautiful theory, and in which knowledge can become more valuable by being given away. At its best, it rewards curiosity, craftsmanship, argument, skepticism, generosity, and a peculiar willingness to let reality win.

It is also, as Burja says near the end of the conversation, a calling. I like that word because it separates science from "scientist" as a job title. A career is something an institution can give or take away. The desire to understand why something happens is much harder to bureaucratize.

What needs fixing, then, is not "science" in this sense. It is the machinery that sometimes confuses measurable outputs with the activity itself. Paper counts are measurable. Citations are measurable. Grant income is measurable. Journal names are measurable. Mentorship, taste, conceptual courage, the ability to synthesize fields, or the capacity to spend six months asking whether everyone has formulated the problem incorrectly are much harder to put in a spreadsheet.

This is almost a Goodhart problem: once a proxy becomes a target, the institution becomes very good at manufacturing the proxy. The danger with AI is that it makes the manufacture of many proxies dramatically cheaper.

If a department previously expected four papers and AI makes four papers easier, one possible future is that everyone gets more time to think. Another is that the department now expects eight.

The second future is the Red Queen future.

I would rather use some of the productivity gain to rebuild the parts of science that are difficult to count: deeper mentorship, more contact between theory and experiment, more replication and synthesis, more room for strange questions, more small groups with enough autonomy to be wrong for a while, and more time in which nothing visibly productive is happening.

In other words, AI could be used to increase throughput. Or it could be used to buy back thought.

8. The garden and the frontier

de Montaigne, Thiel, and Burja are not giving the same advice. I would not want to force them into a synthetic philosophy that, in addition, I am incapable to synthesize right now, either way. But together they illuminate three different failures.

de Montaigne warns us, involuntarily, about allowing the public game to consume the private self. Thiel warns about mistaking crowded competition for importance. Burja warns, in a different register, that a living practice can be replaced by a bureaucratic representation of that practice. The little model above adds a fourth warning: even a tool that makes every individual more capable can produce disappointing collective outcomes when the standards of competition move location.

None of this is an argument against AI. I use AI. The capability gain is real and tangible. The question is what we do with the gain.

Let me approach this a la de Montaigne. If an hour of calculation becomes five minutes, do I receive fifty-five minutes of life back? Do I use them to read something difficult, talk to a colleague or a student, stare at a problem, learn a neighboring field, obtain a competitive advantage for my company, walk, or do absolutely nothing for a while?

Or does the institution quietly convert those fifty-five minutes into another calculation, another figure, another manuscript, another referee report, another benchmark, another metric? This is more likely the case, given that everybody is reporting a burn out.

The first possibility is a technological dividend that brings us to another equilibrium. The second is an arms race.

Perhaps the most useful response is therefore neither anti-AI nor accelerationist. It is to become more deliberate about which games deserve acceleration. This is where we were essentially before the AI, simply because doing calculations or putting an effort required.. effort.

There are parts of science or technology that should become faster. There are also parts that probably should not. Understanding is not identical to throughput. A new idea is not the same thing as a larger PDF, etc.

de Montaigne wanted an arrière-boutique. Thiel wants a monopoly. I think a scientist needs something closer to the first: a small region of intellectual life in which the incentive gradients are weak enough that one can still notice what is interesting.
