Montaigne, Peter Thiel, and the Red Queen effect

August 20th, 2026

A few years ago I left my position at Los Alamos. In retrospect, it was a good thing, and my decision was ultimately based on a form of cost-benefit analysis. At the time, however, it did not feel like one. Leaving a prestigious institution is strange because prestige has a peculiar gravitational field. From the inside, one can be unhappy, uncertain, or simply aware that something is no longer working. From the outside, leaving looks irrational. There are positions one is supposed to want. Once one has obtained one, voluntarily walking away can feel less like a decision and more like stepping off a ledge.

I have been thinking about this recently after encountering, almost by accident, a few very different discussions. I was reading about the life of Michel de Montaigne. The algorithm then recommended Peter Thiel's famous Stanford lecture, Competition Is for Losers. Only afterward did I notice that the person introducing him was Sam Altman. Yuck.

The surprising thing is that Montaigne and Thiel are, in a very loose sense, saying something similar: get out of the rat race.

But they mean almost opposite things by it. Montaigne wants to recover a life that is not exhausted by status and public performance. Thiel wants to stop competing on dimensions chosen by everybody else and occupy a position in which comparison itself becomes difficult. One retreats from the game; the other changes the game.

While writing this post I also watched this conversation with Samo Burja about why science may have slowed down . I do not agree with every claim in it, but it sharpened the question I was already asking: what is genuinely good about science as a human activity, and which parts of the modern scientific machinery are merely proxies that have slowly become confused with science itself?

The current AI boom makes the question more urgent because it is producing a new kind of rat race. AI can increase what an individual scientist is able to do. But if everybody receives the same kind of acceleration, the equilibrium response may simply be that everybody is expected to run faster.

1. Montaigne decided to leave the race

I have been reading about Montaigne because I saw a few analogies with myself. Michel de Montaigne was born in 1533 into a wealthy family near Bordeaux (unlike mine). He became a magistrate and eventually served in the Parlement of Bordeaux. These were not marginal positions in sixteenth-century France. They belonged to precisely the type world of status, office, political connections, reputation, and public advancement that ambitious people were expected to pursue.

Then, in 1571, around the age of thirty-eight, Montaigne withdrew from his judicial career. (Funnily enough, this is roughly the same age at which I decided to quit my LANL job.)

He returned to his family estate, made the tower library his intellectual headquarters, and began writing what became the Essais. The word essai in French means an attempt, a trial, an experiment. Montaigne was not constructing a philosophical system in the manner of someone trying to establish the final architecture of reality. He was trying things out on himself.

His subject was, famously, Montaigne himself, e.g. he became pensive, introspective - maybe as a form of healing, I would add, but that is beyond the point. In this sense he can feel like a proto-psychologist, a proto-anthropologist, and a proto-sociologist, well before any of those fields existed. He tried to understand the human condition by observing one particularly convenient specimen: himself.

This can sound narcissistic until one reads him. What makes Montaigne important is almost the opposite. He distrusts grandiosity. He distrusts certainty. He distrusts the strange human tendency to turn one's position in a social hierarchy into the measure of one's existence.

His famous question was Que sais-je? — what do I know?

This was written in a century saturated with people who were quite certain that they knew. Europe was being torn apart by political and religious conflict. Montaigne's response was not indifference, but epistemic modesty. Human beings are fallible, embodied, inconsistent creatures. Wisdom begins partly by noticing this.

What I find especially attractive is his idea of the arrière-boutique, literally the back room behind the shop: an interior place that does not belong completely to one's profession, public identity, obligations, family role, or status. We should reserve some part of ourselves that cannot be fully colonized by the world outside. AI makes me feel that, right now, a form of arrière-boutiqueism is needed.

His retirement should not be romanticized as permanent disappearance from public life. Montaigne later travelled and served two terms as mayor of Bordeaux. The important point is that he created a distance between the "office" and the "self". He could return to public life without allowing public life to become the entirety of his identity.

There is a useful contrast here with modernity. The scientific revolution did not put humans at the cosmological center of the universe; in an important sense it displaced us from it. The industrial revolution, however, enormously increased our practical power over the world. AI does something stranger still, to which I will come back later: it expands individual cognitive reach while simultaneously making the individual human contribution feel less central.

Dostoevsky explores a related theme in Crime and Punishment. One line that has stayed with me is the accusation that the worst sin is to have "destroyed and betrayed yourself for nothing." The point is not that one should never compromise. It is that there is something particularly empty about sacrificing oneself to a game one did not even choose.

Near the end of his life, Montaigne's ideal was wonderfully unheroic: he wanted death to find him planting cabbages, unconcerned with death and even more unconcerned with the unfinished state of his garden. I recently bought a piece of land with something like that idea in mind. How many parallels.

There is something almost offensive about this attitude to modern professional culture. The garden is unfinished. The work is unfinished. The CV is unfinished. The career is unfinished. This refusal to require completion before life can count as successful is almost a small rebellion.

2. Peter Thiel also leaves the competition

The discussion above is not meant as a historical comparison. Montaigne was a sixteenth-century French essayist. If you don't happen to know him, Peter Thiel is an investor and entrepreneur. Famous for the PayPal mafia, at the beginning of his life, with others (including Musk, who had there a more marginal role than often assumed). One may dislike Thiel's politics, his style, or the social world he represents. I am not trying to make you like Peter Thiel.

His claim that "competition is for losers" is usually read as a statement about monopoly. At the business level, that is exactly what it is. A company operating in near-perfect competition has little ability to capture the value it creates. A company that does something genuinely unique can occupy a market in which direct comparison becomes much less meaningful.

But the more interesting part of the lecture comes near the end, when Thiel stops talking about firms and starts talking about people.

Competition, he argues, is psychologically attractive because it validates us. Let me try to make use an academic analogy. If many intelligent people are trying to enter the same profession, win the same fellowship, publish in the same journal, enter the same university, or solve the same fashionable problem, then pursuing it feels important. The crowd supplies the proof.

In business, Thiel recommends starting with a market so narrow that a company can dominate it and only later expanding outward. The scientific analogue is easy to see. One can become the person associated with a small problem, a small method, a small community, and then expand the circle. "I am the only theoretical memristive-device person in Pisa" is not a business plan, but it has the same type of vibe to what Thiel describes. He used the example of the only "British restaurant in Palo Alto".

What struck me especially was his discussion of scientists. Scientists, he suggests, are often motivated less by money than by recognition from their peers. From one point of view that is admirable. Science would be much worse if money were the only motive. But a desire for peer recognition is also exploitable. Prestige can become a currency even when nobody calls it one.

Thiel's objection is that human beings are intensely mimetic. He uses the idea in a Girardian sense; René Girard was one of his teachers at Stanford. We look around, discover what other ambitious people desire, and begin desiring it ourselves. Eventually we become extremely good at climbing ladders without remembering why that particular wall deserved a ladder.

He tells the story of leaving a prestigious New York law firm after seven months and three days. A colleague told him that seeing Thiel leave was reassuring because he had not realized escape was possible. The joke is that escape required nothing more complicated than walking through the front door and not returning.

Montaigne and Thiel are therefore saying superficially similar things for very different purposes. Montaigne says: stop needing to win. Preserve a part of yourself that the game cannot price. Thiel says: stop playing games in which winning means doing slightly better than everybody standing next to you.

Montaigne withdraws from competition because life is finite. Thiel withdraws from competition because competition produces sameness. Conventional accounts of capitalism celebrate competition; Thiel's Silicon Valley strategy is precisely to escape it by building something monopolistic enough that nobody else is in the room.

One wants to leave competition to enjoy life. The other wants to avoid competition to take the market. In that sense, Thiel is a more ruthless Montaigne.

3. AI moves the scientific distribution

These two perspectives matter because they offer two different responses to the way AI is entering science. The Montaignean response is to protect some part of one's intellectual life from the race. The Thielian response is to search for problems on which one is not forced into direct competition with a thousand nearly identical researchers. In both cases, however, it is important to understand the meaning or the context of the world "competing".

Whatever one thinks about the broader effects of AI on humankind, it has already changed the effective level at which an individual researcher can operate. One can move much more quickly through an unfamiliar literature. Symbolic calculations that once consumed an afternoon can be checked in minutes. Code can be written, translated, debugged, and explained rapidly. One can interrogate methods from a neighboring field without first spending six months becoming locally fluent in its dialect.

The natural picture is that the distribution of effective scientific capability moves to the right, where the horizontal axis now is capability. That sounds unambiguously good, and locally it often is.

But there is a new equilibrium problem hiding inside the obvious productivity gain.

Science is not carried out by one isolated researcher receiving a better tool while everyone else remains fixed. Everyone receives the tool, although not equally and not at exactly the same time. Authors adapt. Referees adapt. Students adapt. Journals adapt. Standards adapt.

So the relevant question is not merely:

$$\text{How much more can I do with AI?}$$

It is also:

$$\text{What happens when everybody can do more?}$$

This is where the problem starts to look like a small statistical-mechanical model. The model below is deliberately a toy model. It is not a measurement of scientific talent or a claim that citations are equivalent to scientific value. Its purpose is to isolate an incentive mechanism.

4. A Gaussian model of scientific accessibility

Let me use a simple model to illustrate the problem. It also applies, in a weaker form, to the scientific publication industry, before the existence of tools such as modern AI.

Let me try to explain this. We assign to every scientist an effective field-specific sophistication level $X$. This is not IQ. It is a compressed proxy containing various generic abilities, such as mathematical preparation, familiarity with the literature, technical experience, conceptual background, judgment, and the other ingredients required to absorb a particular piece of work.

Suppose, for simplicity, that this level is Gaussian — the spherical-cow approximation:

$$X\sim\mathcal N(\mu,\sigma^2).$$

Now assign a paper a technical depth $d$. Imagine first a sharp absorption threshold: a researcher can productively use the paper only when

$$X\geq d.$$

This is obviously an idealization. Understanding is not binary, and different papers demand different kinds of expertise. But the threshold makes the mechanism transparent.

Let

$$z=\frac{d-\mu}{\sigma}$$

denote how many standard deviations the paper sits above the mean. The fraction of the scientific population capable of absorbing the paper is then

$$A(d)=1-\Phi(z) =1-\Phi\!\left(\frac{d-\mu}{\sigma}\right).$$

We now introduce another quantity, $q(d)$, denoting the intrinsic scientific value of a contribution of depth $d$, conditional on somebody actually being able to understand and use it. If there are $N$ potential readers, a deliberately crude model of realized recognition is

$$C(d)=Nq(d) \left[ 1-\Phi\!\left(\frac{d-\mu}{\sigma}\right) \right].$$

The formula says mathematically something much simpler: i.e. that there are two competing effects. First, increasing $d$ may increase the scientific value of the work. But it simultaneously moves the paper farther into the upper tail of the population capable of reading it.

I often tell colleagues some version of the following: a very good paper that nobody can absorb may be scientifically valuable, but it is badly positioned for recognition (or citations). That does not mean that the optimal paper must sit exactly at the peak of the sophistication distribution. The optimum depends on how quickly scientific value grows with depth relative to how quickly accessibility disappears.

Now taking logarithms both sides,

$$\log C(d) = \log N+\log q(d) +\log\left[1-\Phi(z)\right].$$

and differentiating with respect to $d$, we obtain

$$ \frac{\partial}{\partial d}\log C(d) = \frac{q'(d)}{q(d)} - \frac{1}{\sigma} \frac{\phi(z)}{1-\Phi(z)}. $$

An important quantity that we will see entering the narrative is the inverse Mills ratio,

$$ m(z)=\frac{\phi(z)}{1-\Phi(z)}. $$

Then

$$ \frac{\partial}{\partial d}\log C(d) = \frac{q'(d)}{q(d)} - \frac{1}{\sigma}m(z). $$

This equation contains a picture of what I am try to describe. The first term is the proportional gain from moving deeper into the scientific frontier. The second is the accessibility penalty: how quickly the potential population of readers disappears as the paper becomes more demanding.

For simplicity, take the particularly simple form

$$q(d)=e^{\gamma d},\qquad \gamma>0.$$

Then

$$\frac{q'(d)}{q(d)}=\gamma,$$

and the recognition-maximizing sophistication $d^*$ satisfies

$$ m(z^*)=\gamma\sigma. $$

Therefore, one obtains the equation for the maximum below,

$$ { d^* = \mu+\sigma m^{-1}(\gamma\sigma) }. $$

The expression above, should be taken with care. There is no universal rule saying that the optimal paper sits one standard deviation above the mean scientist. However, this is what I kept telling people in words, and now I'm using a formal argument for it. Nonetheless, the location depends on the scientific return to depth, $\gamma$, and on the width of the sophistication distribution, $\sigma$.

The model gives a distinction that is often lost when we talk casually about "better science." Scientific quality and scientific recognition are not the same object. Recognition is quality filtered through a population capable of absorbing the work. Tails may be for the brave rather than the careerist. The deeper question is what we do science for. It also serves as a good baseline for the argument that I would like to make below, which is the red queen effect when AI is added to the picture.

5. A Red Queen effect

We now try to repeat the same argument as before, while introducing a tool that improves the capability of the single researcher, i.e. we introduce AI. The simplest possible assumption is that AI increases the effective sophistication of every scientist by a fixed amount $a$ (and this amount depends on the model generation):

$$X^{AI}=X+a.$$

Under this assumption the distribution shifts to the right,

$$X^{AI}\sim\mathcal N(\mu+a,\sigma^2),$$

but its width does not change. This detail will matter later: the toy model says nothing by itself about whether AI makes real scientific capability more equal or more unequal.

A paper of depth $d$ can now be absorbed when

$$X+a\geq d,$$

or equivalently

$$X\geq d-a.$$

Following the same analysis as before, the accessible fraction becomes

$$ A(d;a) = 1-\Phi\!\left( \frac{d-a-\mu}{\sigma} \right). $$

With $q(d)=e^{\gamma d}$,

$$ C(d;a) = N e^{\gamma d} \left[ 1-\Phi\!\left( \frac{d-a-\mu}{\sigma} \right) \right]. $$

We now define

$$ z=\frac{d-a-\mu}{\sigma}. $$

and repeating the derivative again, we obtain

$$ \frac{\partial}{\partial d}\log C = \gamma-\frac{1}{\sigma}m(z). $$

The optimum therefore obeys the same standardized condition,

$$m(z^*)=\gamma\sigma.$$

Hence $z^*$ is constant and

$$ \boxed{ d^*(a) = a+\mu+\sigma m^{-1}(\gamma\sigma) }. $$

Consequently,

$$ { \frac{\partial d^*}{\partial a}=1 }. $$

This is the result I find both beautiful, but slightly disturbing.

If AI gives everyone one additional unit of effective scientific capability, the recognition-maximizing paper becomes one unit more sophisticated.

The relevant gap is

$$d^*(a)-a,$$

and

$$ d^*(a)-a = \mu+\sigma m^{-1}(\gamma\sigma), $$

which does not depend on $a$. Therefore the accessible fraction at the moving optimum is also unchanged:

$$ A(d^*(a);a) = 1-\Phi\!\left[m^{-1}(\gamma\sigma)\right]. $$

In words, everybody becomes more capable.

The endogenous frontier moves by exactly the same amount.

Nobody becomes relatively closer to it. There is no "we will now publish PRLs". PRL will adapt to accept papers shifted in quality by the AI. Consequently, who does things "old style", by hand, will necessarily be worse off.

This is a scientific Red Queen effect: we run faster in order to remain in the same place. In biology, more precisely, the red queen effect or hypothesis, is that species must constantly adapt and evolve, just to remain in the same spot in relation to the other species, see for instance the Wikipedia place. The effect is inspired by the Red Queen in Lewis Carroll story, Through the looking glass: "Now, here, you see, it takes all the running you can do, to keep in the same place.".

Notice what did not happen. The model does not say AI is useless. At any fixed paper depth $d$, raising $a$ makes the paper more accessible. The disappearance of that gain occurs only because scientific production responds endogenously and moves $d$ as well.

The mistake would be to hold the frontier fixed while changing the capabilities of the people competing near it.

6. A tragedy of the scientific commons

The previous result is not yet a tragedy. It is an arms race in which a relative gain is dissipated because everybody has the same gain. The stronger possibility appears when we remember that scientific attention is scarce, or when not everybody has access to the same AI advantage.

Knowledge itself is not a rival resource. My understanding of an equation does not prevent you from understanding it. Attention is different. There are only so many papers a person can read, talks one can attend, manuscripts one can referee, and ideas one can seriously think about.

Suppose there are $M$ papers, and paper $i$ has depth $d_i$. Let its share of scientific attention be

$$ v_i = \frac{e^{\rho(a)d_i}} {\sum_{j=1}^{M}e^{\rho(a)d_j}}. $$

The parameter $\rho(a)$ measures how strongly apparent frontier sophistication attracts attention. This is a modeling assumption, not a claim that every scientific community rewards complexity this way. It simply captures a recognizable incentive: technical sophistication can itself become a signal of frontier status.

Thus, the realized impact is now

$$ Y_i = N e^{\gamma d_i} \left[ 1-\Phi\!\left( \frac{d_i-a-\mu}{\sigma} \right) \right]v_i. $$

Now an author chooses $d_i$. Differentiating the log-attention term gives

$$ \frac{\partial\log v_i}{\partial d_i} = \rho(a)(1-v_i). $$

The private first-order condition is therefore

$$ \gamma - \frac{1}{\sigma} m\!\left( \frac{d_i-a-\mu}{\sigma} \right) + \rho(a)(1-v_i) = 0. $$

In a symmetric equilibrium,

$$v_i=\frac{1}{M}.$$

If we define

$$ R(a)=\rho(a)\left(1-\frac{1}{M}\right). $$

The Nash condition becomes

$$ \frac{1}{\sigma} m\!\left( \frac{d_N-a-\mu}{\sigma} \right) = \gamma+R(a). $$

Or, equivalently, we have

$$ m(z_N) = \sigma[\gamma+R(a)], $$

and from which it follows

$$ { d_N(a) = a+\mu+ \sigma m^{-1}\!\left( \sigma[\gamma+R(a)] \right) }. $$

This is where the "commons" language becomes literal, at least in this model. Suppose all authors could coordinate and increase their depths together. In a symmetric move, every $d_i$ rises by the same amount and therefore every attention share remains exactly $1/M$. The attention term gives the group no collective benefit at all.

A coordinated symmetric optimum therefore satisfies the old condition

$$ m(z_C)=\gamma\sigma, $$

and hence

$$ d_C(a) = a+\mu+\sigma m^{-1}(\gamma\sigma). $$

But each individual author, moving alone, can steal a little attention by increasing $d_i$. That private incentive contributes the extra $R(a)$ term. Since the Gaussian inverse Mills ratio is increasing,

$$R(a)>0\quad\Longrightarrow\quad d_N(a)>d_C(a).$$

In other words, the Nash equilibrium contains more complexity than the coordinated equilibrium. Everyone pays the readability cost; nobody keeps the relative attention advantage once everyone has responded. Thus, even at the level of the attention, we still have a red queen effect.

We can now ask what happens if AI itself makes the competition for attention more intense. This is an additional assumption, encoded by $R'(a)>0$; it does not follow automatically from AI.

Starting from

$$ m(z_N)=\sigma[\gamma+R(a)], $$

implicit differentiation gives

$$ m'(z_N)\frac{dz_N}{da} = \sigma R'(a), $$

and therefore

$$ \frac{dz_N}{da} = \frac{\sigma R'(a)}{m'(z_N)}. $$

Since

$$d_N=a+\mu+\sigma z_N,$$

we obtain

$$ \boxed{ \frac{\mathrm d d_N}{\mathrm d a} = 1+ \frac{\sigma^2R'(a)}{m'(z_N)} }. $$

For the Gaussian inverse Mills ratio,

$$ m'(z)=m(z)[m(z)-z]>0. $$

Thus, if

$$R'(a)>0,$$

then

$$ { \frac{\mathrm d d_N}{\mathrm d a}>1. } $$

This is now stronger than the Red Queen result. The frontier no longer moves to the right at the same speed as effective capability. It moves effectvely faster.

The standardized gap is

$$ z_N= \frac{d_N-a-\mu}{\sigma}. $$

When $R'(a)>0$, we have $dz_N/da>0$, and therefore

$$ \frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm d a}[1-\Phi(z_N)] = -\phi(z_N)\frac{dz_N}{da} \le 0. $$

We have arrived at the paradox:

AI can make every scientist individually more capable while a competitive equilibrium makes the scientific literature relatively less accessible.

An individual scientist may gain an advantage by producing something that looks more technically advanced: another derivation, another simulation, another model, another layer of formal machinery. If only one person does this, perhaps that person obtains more attention. If everybody does it, the relative advantage disappears. What remains is the additional complexity.

This is precisely where Thiel re-enters the story. Competition makes us better at the dimension on which we are competing. The uncomfortable question is whether that dimension was worth optimizing in the first place.

AI may make the problem severe because it lowers the cost of participating in the race. If everyone can generate more derivations, more code, more simulations, more literature review, more polished prose, and more papers, the equilibrium response need not be leisure. It may simply be that the expected quantity of all those things rises.

A few posts ago I speculated that AI might flatten some scientific capability differences. This toy model cannot establish that. By construction, adding the same $a$ to everybody shifts the mean and leaves $\sigma$ unchanged. In the real world, unequal access, unequal skill at using AI, compute, data, institutional support, and reputation could widen the distribution instead. That is an empirical question, not a consequence of the equations above.

7. Science is not a KPI

This is where the Samo Burja conversation I mentioned at the beginning became useful to me.

Burja's claim is that science may have slowed down, and that the explanation is not simply that humanity picked all the low-hanging fruit. He distinguishes scientific progress from technological progress: making a machine larger, faster, or cheaper can be a genuine technological achievement without necessarily giving us a new theory of why nature works the way it does.

I am not convinced by every example, and "science has slowed down" is much too broad a sentence to be accepted without qualifications. Different fields behave differently. Instrumentation matters. Large collaborations can discover things that solitary geniuses simply cannot. Modern peer review, grant systems, and institutions also exist for reasons: science at scale needs error correction, accountability, continuity, and ways of distributing scarce resources.

But one part of the discussion struck me. Burja argues that science depends on a loop between theory and empirical observation, and that some of the most important scientific abilities are difficult to transmit as formal instructions. A textbook can teach you the result. It is much harder for it to teach you what he calls the "generators": the habits of mind that let somebody invent the question, choose the right approximation, notice the anomaly, or decide which ugly calculation is worth doing.

This is tacit knowledge. It is one reason mentorship matters. It is also one reason the separation between theoreticians and experimentalists can occasionally become unhealthy: the person who understands the theory may not be close enough to the apparatus to notice what the apparatus is trying to say, while the person running the apparatus may be too far from the theory to know which tiny deviation is conceptually explosive.

There is something very nice about science when this loop works. Science is one of the few human activities in which being wrong can be productive, in which a sufficiently stubborn fact can humiliate a beautiful theory, and in which knowledge can become more valuable by being given away. At its best, it rewards curiosity, craftsmanship, argument, skepticism, generosity, and a peculiar willingness to let reality win.

It is also, as Burja says near the end of the conversation, a calling. I like that word because it separates science from "scientist" as a job title. A career is something an institution can give or take away. The desire to understand why something happens is much harder to bureaucratize.

What needs fixing, then, is not "science" in this sense. It is the machinery that sometimes confuses measurable outputs with the activity itself. Paper counts are measurable. Citations are measurable. Grant income is measurable. Journal names are measurable. Mentorship, taste, conceptual courage, the ability to synthesize fields, or the capacity to spend six months asking whether everyone has formulated the problem incorrectly are much harder to put in a spreadsheet.

This is almost a Goodhart problem: once a proxy becomes a target, the institution becomes very good at manufacturing the proxy. The danger with AI is that it makes the manufacture of many proxies dramatically cheaper.

If a department previously expected four papers and AI makes four papers easier, one possible future is that everyone gets more time to think. Another is that the department now expects eight.

The second future is the Red Queen future.

I would rather use some of the productivity gain to rebuild the parts of science that are difficult to count: deeper mentorship, more contact between theory and experiment, more replication and synthesis, more room for strange questions, more small groups with enough autonomy to be wrong for a while, and more time in which nothing visibly productive is happening.

In other words, AI could be used to increase throughput. Or it could be used to buy back thought.

8. The garden and the frontier

Montaigne, Thiel, and Burja are not giving the same advice. I would not want to force them into a synthetic philosophy that, in addition, I am incapable to synthesize right now, either way.

But together they illuminate three different failures.

Montaigne warns about allowing the public game to consume the private self. Thiel warns about mistaking crowded competition for importance. Burja warns, in a different register, that a living practice can be replaced by a bureaucratic representation of that practice. The little model above adds a fourth warning: even a tool that makes every individual more capable can produce disappointing collective outcomes when the standards of competition move location.

None of this is an argument against AI. I use AI. The capability gain is real and tangible. The question is what we do with the gain.

If an hour of calculation becomes five minutes, do I receive fifty-five minutes of life back? Do I use them to read something difficult, talk to a colleague or a student, stare at a problem, learn a neighboring field, obtain a competitive advantage for my company, walk, or do absolutely nothing for a while?

Or does the institution quietly convert those fifty-five minutes into another calculation, another figure, another manuscript, another referee report, another benchmark, another metric? This is more likely the case, given that everybody is reporting a burn out.

The first possibility is a technological dividend that brings us to another equilibrium. The second is an arms race.

Perhaps the most useful response is therefore neither anti-AI nor accelerationist. It is to become more deliberate about which games deserve acceleration. This is where we were essentially before the AI, simply because doing calculations or putting an effort required.. effort.

There are parts of science or technology that should become faster. There are also parts that probably should not. Understanding is not identical to throughput. A new idea is not the same thing as a larger PDF, etc.

Montaigne wanted an arrière-boutique. Thiel wants a monopoly. I think a scientist needs something closer to the first: a small region of intellectual life in which the incentive gradients are weak enough that one can still notice what is interesting.
