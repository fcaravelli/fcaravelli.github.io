The great painters and photography

October 25th, 2026

A keynote about programmers and painters, a French philosopher's shopping list for the soul, and a few thoughts on what AI is doing to the people who do calculations for a living.

1. David Heinemeier Hansson and the painters of the past

A few weeks ago, David Heinemeier Hansson gave the opening keynote at RailsConf. He is a very well known programmer, the inventor of Ruby on Rails and, in my opinion, a very good speaker and motivator – and a race car driver. This was an awaited keynote, as currently any programmer is going through a very difficult time, with the rise of AI and the fear of being replaced by it.

Now, I have been using computers since I was 8 years old. I vividly remember my dad bringing home a 386 computer, I think a Deskpro. I don't know exactly what he used it for, but it was a great machine for its time, and he was running on it the early software for electrical engineering. For me it was a rather mysterious object. Windows did not exist yet – it would arrive in the mid 90s – and the computer was running MS-DOS. Fast forward a few years, and I was using computers for programming, initially in Visual Basic. Let me say that I have never been a great programmer; my interest shifted to Physics when I was around 15.

Around 2000, at the turn of the century, my friend Pietro, who lived roughly 200 meters from me, and I were told that we were not supposed to use the telephone to communicate, as this was costly. There was no "data plan" yet, and you paid per minute. We were probably 13 or 14 years old. He was an electronics enthusiast, and he had a computer at home. I was more interested in programming at the time, and I told him: let's build a device to communicate with each other. He custom built a device with an antenna, I wrote the software to talk to it and decode the signal. To my surprise, the device worked. We never managed to reach the range we needed to actually talk to each other, but we were able to communicate over a few meters. This was my first experience with "making things happen". There is a particularly rewarding feeling when you build something that works. I went on to study Physics, while he went on to study electronics.

I have always been a computer enthusiast, although this was essentially on pause between 2004 and 2016, a hiatus due to my interest in (theoretical) Physics. Around 2016 I started to get interested in memristors (for reasons I will get into in a future post), and I got back into programming and electronics, in order to make theoretical studies and predictions about the behavior of many of these components. Every year I say to myself: this is the last year I work on memristive devices, and every year I discover something new and exciting. It is a nice intersection between physics, complex systems, computing and electronics, and programming these systems has nothing to do with "programming" per se.

Going back to the keynote, DHH made a very interesting parallel between the great painters at the time of the introduction of photography (go here in his talk to see it) and the current situation of programmers with the rise of AI. Until roughly the middle of the nineteenth century, painters were the only way to capture a moment in time. The upper bourgeoisie and the royal families would commission painters to paint their portraits, and the painters would be paid handsomely for their work, spending months on it. It must also have been horrific to be a painter at the time, as you would have to paint the same person over and over again. Then photography was invented, and between the early 1900s and the 1930s it became cheaper and cheaper, a perfect example of how technological change affects society. While still expensive – $1 in 1900 is worth roughly $40 today (check here) – it was faster and cheaper than hiring a painter.

People still paint today. Indeed, the great painters either switched to photography or moved where photography could not follow: cubism and the abstract art movement were, in a way, a reaction to the advent of photography, painters trying to find a new way to express themselves without relying on strict realism. Similarly, the advent of computers in chess and then in go did not stop people from playing these games. However, these are, except for a few people, a hobby. It is very hard to make money with either of them unless you are one of the very best players in the world. The same was not true for painters, and is not true for programmers: these were, and are, professions. This is the uncomfortable part of the parallel.

2. Simone Weil and the needs of the soul

Ironically, in the same weeks I have been reading Simone Weil's work, collected in An Anthology. Weil was a French philosopher, and she wrote about the needs of the soul. The best way I can describe her work is: profound, and a very fine thinker. She was born in 1909 and died in 1943, at the age of 34. She was very interested in philosophy, mathematics and physics, which transpires from her writings, and she was deeply concerned with social issues and the plight of the working class. Unlike the theorists of her time, she went to work in a factory to share that condition first hand; later, under the German occupation, she worried about the fate of the Jews and of the French people. She was a very interesting person, and I highly recommend reading her work.

Anyhow, in The Need for Roots she wrote what is essentially a shopping list of the needs of the soul. It is worth going through it one need at a time, because every single item on the list has been touched, and mostly not for the better, by what has happened in society in the last twenty years.

The first need is order: essentially the social texture that surrounds the individual and gives a sense of belonging. Our societal foundations have been essentially destroyed. The extended family, the neighbourhood, the workplace that lasted a lifetime, have all been thinned out in the span of a generation, and we have replaced them with feeds. A feed is not a texture. Then comes liberty, intended as the ability to act and think freely, without being constrained by external forces. Formally, we have never been freer; in practice, the space in which one can act and think without being nudged, tracked and ranked has shrunk considerably. The external force is no longer a king or a priest, it is an algorithm that knows what you are going to do before you do it. Obedience, for Weil, is not servility: it is the ability to follow rules and norms and to tell right from wrong, and it presupposes rules that are legitimate and people who answer for them. I liked the sentence: "Thus anybody politically governed by a man who responds to nobody is in the hands of a sick man." We have an abundance of rules and a scarcity of people who answer for anything, and the man who responds to nobody is now, quite often, not even a man but a platform.

Responsibility is the ability to take responsibility for one's actions and their consequences, and this is an interesting one. Having a family as an adult implies responsibilities under uncertainty, and we do not want those: the age at which people take them on keeps moving forward, or does not arrive at all. We have built a society in which responsibility is postponed, delegated or insured against, and then we complain that nobody feels responsible for anything. Equality, for Weil, consists in a form of public recognition in which we are treated equally, and thus provides a sense of fairness. That recognition is exactly what goes missing when the rules seem to apply differently depending on who you are, who you know, or how loudly you can complain; the perception of fairness has eroded faster than any statistic about it. Hierarchy is a sense of devotion to higher figures, which provides guidance. Devotion presupposes figures worth being devoted to, and we have replaced hierarchy with celebrity. Nobody is guided by a celebrity. Honour is the need to respect other human beings and to be respected in turn. Respect is hard to give and to receive through a screen: much of our interaction today is anonymous, and anonymity does to honour what darkness does to manners.

Punishment, in Weil's list, is a need and not a threat: the soul needs to be able to distinguish right from wrong and to accept punishment for wrongdoing, because punishment, accepted, reintegrates. We do not accept punishment even when we know we were in the wrong, and parents do not allow their children to be punished. A society that cannot punish cannot forgive either. Freedom of opinion is the ability to express one's opinion freely, without fear of reprisal (she lists freedom of association as a separate need). We have more channels than ever to express an opinion, and more consequences than ever for expressing the wrong one: you are free to say what you think, provided you are prepared to be fired for it.

Security and risk come as a pair. Security is not being under the weight of terror; risk is needed because its absence produces boredom. Most of us are not under the weight of terror, and this is a genuine achievement of the last eighty years. In its place, though, we have a low-grade, permanent anxiety about work, money and the next crisis, and it is not obvious that the soul is better off. At the same time we have removed risk from everything: everything is insured, moderated, age-restricted and comes with a disclaimer, and we have become, collectively, dull.

Private property and collective property come as a pair as well. We need to be surrounded by objects that we can call our own, and we need to feel that the public things – the squares, the monuments, the institutions – are in part ours. On the first, the slogan of the last decade goes: "you will own nothing and you will be happy". We rent our music, our software, our films, and increasingly our houses and our cars; Weil would have said that a man who owns nothing has nothing to anchor himself to. On the second, when protests end with monuments defaced and public buildings destroyed, it is a sign that people no longer feel these things are theirs. There is no respect for institutions or for other people's belongings, and this kind of rage does not come from nowhere: it comes from not belonging to the thing being destroyed.

Last comes truth: falsehood is a source of suffering, and knowledge anchors the soul. This is what certain people have been eroding since the beginning of social media. Truth has become one option among many, something to be optimized for engagement rather than for correctness, and generative AI, which produces plausible falsehood at zero marginal cost, arrives right on time to finish the job.

3. Cycles, technology and technofeudalism

The discussion about the needs for the soul and how these were attacked one by one by social media show that these are interesting times. Two other books I have read recently are The Prophets of Doom by Neema Parvini and The Turning Point by Steve Richards (not to be confused with Capra's The Turning Point, another beautiful book that I suggest if you are into scientific literature, by the same author of The Tao of Physics). The first is a very interesting survey of the thinkers who, against the dominant linear and progressive view of history, saw it as cyclical, and who predicted many of the maladies we are living through. The second is a very interesting analysis of the roughly 80-year patterns in historical events, from growth to crisis, in four parts of roughly 20 years each. I think that, even if a clear acceleration in technological innovation is altering the way in which these cycles unfold, we are right on track for a major economic, cultural and identity crisis.

Michel Foucault calls epistemes the particular configurations of knowledge of a culture at a given point in time and space; with each comes a particular way of thinking, a vision of the world. Thatcher and Reagan, for instance, could be associated with financialization and globalization, and the rise of the left in the 20th century with the rise of the welfare state. Yet, as in many cases, cultural trends intertwine with economic and political ones, and it is hard to separate them. Varoufakis calls our time the one of technofeudalism. Going back to the needs of the soul, the prelude to technofeudalism has destroyed many of them and replaced them with a kind of digital nihilism, and the rise of AI is only going to accelerate the process. The next 20 years of this cultural change are going to require us to be even more desperate than we are right now. As Žižek says, only when you are desperate do you act.

Now, technology has always been part of the human condition, for better or for worse. Give me a lever and I will move the world, said Archimedes. The parabolic motion of projectiles, believe it or not, discovered by Galileo, was a technological innovation that allowed for the development of artillery, and thus the rise of the modern state, whether for defense or for offense. It is true that it is the gun that shoots the bullet, but it is the person who pulls the trigger who is responsible for the action. The same is true for AI, and it is up to us to use it for the best or for the worst. Wider availability of technology has always been a double-edged sword. Technology shifts Nash equilibria; this is why non-proliferation exists. Yet, with AI, we are giving the keys of hell to everybody. Lasciate ogni speranza, voi ch'entrate, said Dante. That is why, maybe, we should not go in at all.

4. 105,000 hours

A cultural shift is also happening in the procedures of science itself, procedures consolidated over centuries (although already perturbed by the introduction of computers). We, theoretical physicists, are undergoing a process of accelerated aging. It used to be the case that as a human ages, his ability to learn, to do calculations, to think carefully, slows down a little, and the focus moves to more important questions, the ones that require a higher level of understanding. AI is accelerating this, in the sense that we are starting to rely much less on our own calculations, similarly to what happens when you start having students and postdocs who do part of the work for you. In national labs this is less pronounced. There are fewer postdocs and students, groups are smaller, and the work is more focused on the individual; you still see older scientists doing calculations on their own at a venerable age. This is not always the case though, and AI "postdocs" are going to bring the process there as well.

I did a rough calculation the other day: how many hours have I spent in front of a computer since I was 13? It came back as roughly 105,000. These things change how you think and how you act. The generation of kids who have used a tablet since they were 5 years old now show a reduction in the number of words they know, in their cognitive abilities and in their ability to focus. This is a cultural shift, and it is going to be very hard to reverse. This is the same generation that will have to face a great deal of adversity, and they will have AI right in their face. The great painters, in a way, had to face this as well, and they decided to move to abstract art, to photography, to other forms of art. We are going to have to do the same, and we are going to have to do it fast.

We will need people who can see around corners. And in truth, adversity will select those who can.

5. The scientific peasantry

Scientists, I don't think, are particularly capable of doing that either. Modern science has been stuck. Either scientists were supposed to be asking the great metaphysical questions, as is typical in the important institutes, or they were supposed to play the role of the scientific peasantry. And sometimes the circles will put you in that box and not let you move out of it, whether you have published 1, 3, 5 or 20 PRLs, or Natures, or Sciences. It has always been that way: there have been the Alti Ministeri, and the lower ones. However, as Weil herself said, it is the slave who makes the master. These high-circle institutions need citations, and we, the peasantry, are the ones providing them. They live by the numbers: the citations, the h-index, the i10-index, or whatever economists will love this decade to measure the productivity of science.

Whether the question of the existence of a direction of time, or of whether 7 is a perfect number or not – the kind of metaphysical question the Rovellis of our time seem so attached to – is important or not, the real question is whether it has any effect on the lives of people. And I do believe that, over a horizon of 300 years, the answer is no. As a matter of fact, I attended a workshop (I will not tell you where, or with whom that conversation was) in which a famous scientist, let us call him CR7, did the unthinkable. Another famous scientist at the same workshop, extremely pleasant and nice, and a much better scientist than CR7 in my personal (but biased) opinion – let us call him CM – told the audience, for reasons I now do not recall, the story of him telling two of his students that maybe there are more important things. On that day, CM had heard the news of the Taliban killing a 12-year-old girl going to school in Afghanistan. Right after he mentioned it (as I recall the exchange), the students went on to tell him about some message passing technique, as if the news meant nothing to them. CM remarked that there are, sometimes, more important things than message passing. CR7, who was sitting there, replied that the Taliban were rightfully protecting their land and minimized the death of the girl. This is what I mean when I say that scientists, even the best, even those who are supposed to be philosophically inclined, can be absolute dumbfucks.

I somewhat (not completely, I still have hopes) lost faith in Academia because Academia lost faith in itself. Blinded by prestige, Academia beshat whatever equivalent of the Hippocratic oath we have in the sciences (this could be Descartes' Discourse on the Method). Merit in arguments can be removed by vocal energy; similarly, merit in prestige can be removed by intellectual energy. Scientists, maybe, need to remember which are the important battles, and which hill is worth dying on. The great painters, when photography arrived, knew.

The issue is that I think the divide between the "top league" and the "scientific peasantry" is going to be exacerbated by AI. The top league will have access to the best AI, and the peasantry will have access to the worst. If the two are given the same tools, the person being a professor at a top university will nonetheless get the credit because of the prestige of the institute. If cases such as Ramanujan were rare before, they will be even rarer now. The peasantry will be left to do the work, and the top league will get the credit. The system was already unfair, and it is going to get worse. Let me just recall that these are problems about human condition, and about science as persevered by human scientists. At the end of the day, Science will be discovered one way or another, just not in the romantic sense of the word anymore.
